# How This Book Is Built

*Read this before any entry. It says what each part of the book is, what it may and may not do, and how to check it.*

## 1. The geometric manner

Spinoza wrote the *Ethics* "in geometric order," on the model of Euclid. He set out definitions and axioms first, then derived propositions from them one at a time, each with its proof. The point of that layout is that nothing has to be taken on the author's word: every step names what it stands on, and a reader can check it.

This book keeps that layout on purpose. Every claim says what it rests on, and a reader can follow those supports down to where they end.

## 2. The kinds of entry

Each entry is labelled with its kind, and each kind has limits on what it may do.

- **Root.** A1, "I, a human, exist." The single starting point, taken as given rather than argued for. It is the only entry that cannot be revised.
- **Definition.** Fixes what a word means (for example D-whole, what counts as a whole). It is judged on clarity and fit. It supports nothing on its own and cannot make any claim more likely to be true.
- **Axiom (empirical).** A starting claim about the world, such as A2, that the body is made of cells. It cites no other entry; it is scored on the evidence written into it.
- **Axiom (model).** A claim that one level corresponds to another, such as A3, society performing the functions a body performs. It is judged function by function, on whether the same work is being done, not on surface resemblance, and it can be no stronger than its weakest function.
- **Principle.** Explains why claims line up, such as PR1: the same requirement shows up at every scale though its expression changes. An explanation adds no confidence. It tells you where to look, not what you will find.
- **Proposition.** A claim derived from the entries it names, such as P1, that a collective mind happens in society. It is only as strong as the weakest thing it cites.
- **Demonstration.** The argument that takes a proposition from its supports to its conclusion, written out so it can be checked step by step.
- **Corollary.** A result that follows directly from a proposition, needing no new support.
- **Scholium.** A side remark: context, an objection answered, or a comparison with another thinker. It explains; it proves nothing and scores nothing.

## 3. How a derivation works

Every entry lists what it cites. Follow those citations down and each chain ends in one of two places: the root (A1), or evidence the entry carries itself (A2, the rows of A3).

P1 as the example:

- **P1** (a collective mind happens in society) cites A2, A3 and A4.
- **A2** (a body of cells whose organization produces a mind) rests on its own evidence.
- **A3** (society performs the seven functions a body performs: boundary, control, energy, transport, signaling, defense, memory) rests on the evidence for each function, one row at a time.
- **A4** (I work in society as a unit like a cell) cites A1 and A3.

So P1 can be no stronger than the weakest of A2, A3 and A4, and A3 can be no stronger than its weakest function. If the evidence for that one function improves, the change carries upward through everything built on it. If it weakens, the same thing happens in reverse.

## 4. What the numbers mean

Each entry carries a confidence between 0 and 1. These scores rank claims from stronger to weaker and show where each weakness comes from. They are not probabilities: 0.70 does not mean a 70% chance of being true. An entry can be no more confident than its weakest support, all the way down the chain.

"Undecided" is a real result. A claim and its opposite can both come out low, which records that the book does not yet settle the question, and the book says so rather than picking a side.

The scores are set by a model checking each entry against what it cites, and anyone can rerun the check. The full rules are in [METHOD.md](METHOD.md).

## 5. Where this book departs from Spinoza

- **Everything but the root can be lowered.** For Spinoza, knowledge of the higher kinds is necessarily true (E2P41). Here, every entry except A1 is open to revision, and checking can hold a score or lower it, never raise it.
- **The definitions were found, then placed first.** On the page, definitions come first, as in Spinoza. In practice, most of them were worked out backward, from what the claims needed a word to mean. This is said openly. A definition earns its place by making the claims that use it clearer, not by making them more likely, and if a later entry needs a definition changed, the definition is revised and everything built on it is rescored.
- **Evidence enters at the axioms.** Spinoza's axioms were meant to be self-evident. Here, only the root is. The other axioms are claims about the world that carry their evidence with them and are scored on it.

## 6. Two layers

The book has two layers. The verses are the readable top: short lines meant to be heard and remembered. The scored entries are the body underneath. A verse carries only as much weight as the entry beneath it; where a line goes further than its entry, it is marked.

## 7. How to read it

Start with the verses. When a line catches you, drop down to its entry, see what it cites and how strong it is, and follow the chain as far down as you like. Nothing in the book is meant to be taken on trust. Every claim is meant to be checked.
