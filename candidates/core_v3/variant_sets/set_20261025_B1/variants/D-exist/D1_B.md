**What exists** *(definition, id: D-exist)*. For activities, to *exist* at a time is nothing more than to be going on then. For anything that is not an activity, "exists" is taken as understood, and D-exist does not define it. "Activity" and "going on" are also taken as understood. A thing *lasts* when it goes on existing through a stretch of time. Spinoza's word for lasting is duration: "Duration is the indefinite continuance of existing" (E2D5).

**Explanation.**

- *What it defines and what it does not.* It defines existence for activities only, and lasting for anything. An earlier wording also said that a thing that is not an activity exists when it is "present". That defined the word by a synonym, so it is withdrawn, and existence for such things is taken as understood. Kant's point stands behind this: "'Being' is obviously not a real predicate; that is, it is not a concept of something which could be added to the concept of a thing" (*Critique of Pure Reason*, A598/B626). The definition does its work by what it leaves out.
- *What it leaves open.* Spinoza draws four distinctions that D-exist does not.
  - *In itself or in something else.* "Everything which exists, exists either in itself or in something else" (E1A1).
  - *By its own nature or not.* Something self-caused is "that of which the essence involves existence" (E1D1), and "Existence belongs to the nature of substances" (E1P7). But "The essence of things produced by God does not involve existence" (E1P24).
  - *For a time or eternally.* Eternity is "existence itself, in so far as it is conceived necessarily to follow solely from the definition of that which is eternal" (E1D8), and "God, and all the attributes of God, are eternal" (E1P19). The earlier wording, "exists at a time" for everything, could not cover that. The present wording ties only activities and lasting to time.
  - *What the thing is.* That is its essence (E2D2), a different question.
- *What A1 uses.* A1 needs only the first clause, at one time: now, while the thinking goes on. Doubting is an activity, so the doubting exists in D-exist's sense whenever it is going on. That is the whole of what the doubt proves. It is also all that Lichtenberg's "it thinks" allows.

**Scholium (why "exist" needs a definition at all).** Without one, A1's "exist" could be read in Spinoza's strongest sense, existence by the necessity of one's own nature. The doubt proves nothing of the kind. D-exist blocks that reading, and keeps A1 claiming exactly what doubting shows. (Spinoza quoted in Elwes 1883 wording, checked against `sources/spinoza/`.)

**Limit.** This is a definition. It carries no confidence and makes no claim more likely.
