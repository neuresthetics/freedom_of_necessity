# Collision: where does the sameness of the I over time belong?

**Status.** A candidate collision, written 2026-09-29 PT at the author's request ("collide it"). It scores nothing and changes nothing in `entries/`. Its result is a new definition of sameness for wholes (D-whole, Definition 12), a thinner statement of what D-I leaves out, and a rewritten P1, step 6.

**The question.** P1 needs the I that is a whole (A2, A3) to be the I that is a member (A4). D-I names only the doer of *this* thought, and A1 holds only "for as long as I am thinking". So where does the sameness of the I from one time to another belong?

- **Thesis S.** It belongs in D-I, as part of what the word "I" means.
- **Thesis B.** It belongs with A2: carried by evidence about what the I is, one body, and not by the definition of a word.

**Rules.** As in [collision_nature.md](collision_nature.md): each side at its strongest, each point naming what it stands on, then a side-by-side judgment on stated criteria.

- **J1.** A1 stays certain.
- **J2.** A definition cannot be false, so nothing that can turn out false goes into one.
- **J3.** The criterion gives answers in the hard cases, and can fail.
- **J4.** Fidelity to the sources, stated where the book departs.
- **J5.** It fits an AI as far as A1 does, and no further.
- **J6.** No aiming words, and no "inside/outside" for the felt side.

Spinoza is quoted in the Elwes 1883 wording, checked against `sources/spinoza/ethics_elwes_1883.txt` and the Latin in `sources/spinoza/ethica_latin.txt`, and cited by part and proposition (standard text Kisner, Cambridge; pages pending).

## Thesis S: sameness belongs in D-I

**S1 (Locke: the I that matters is a person).** Locke puts personal identity in consciousness: "as far as this consciousness can be extended backwards to any past action or thought, so far reaches the identity of that person" (*Essay* II.xxvii.9). And "person" is "a forensic term, appropriating actions and their merit; and so belongs only to intelligent agents, capable of a law, and happiness, and misery" (II.xxvii.26). A book about freedom will speak of what I did and answer for. A D-I that names only the doer of one thought defines a momentary doer, not a person. So sameness, by memory, should be in the meaning of the word.

**S2 (Spinoza's Spanish poet).** Spinoza himself denies that a living body is enough for sameness. "I consider that a body undergoes death, when the proportion of motion and rest which obtained mutually among its several parts is changed," even while "keeping the circulation of the blood". He tells of "a certain Spanish poet" who recovered from an illness but "remained so oblivious of his past life, that he would not believe the plays and tragedies he had written to be his own", and adds that "a man undergoes such changes, that I should hardly call him the same" (E4P39, Scholium; Latin *ut non facile eundem illum esse dixerim*). So "one lasting body", the phrase A2 and P1 used after the second round, gives the wrong answer by Spinoza's own lights. The poet's body lasted.

**S3 (identity across premises is a matter of meaning).** A demonstration equivocates if one term names different things in different premises. P1's validity, and not only its truth, depends on the "I" of A2 being the "I" of A4. Validity should be settled by meaning. If sameness is left to evidence, whether P1 is even a valid argument becomes an empirical question.

**S4 (Descartes: sameness is as evident as existence).** "Is it not one and the same 'I' who is now doubting almost everything, who nonetheless understands some things, who affirms that this one thing is true ...? ... The fact that it is I who am doubting and understanding and willing is so evident that I see no way of making it any clearer" (Second Meditation, AT VII 28–29; CSM II 19). And the body is a poor bearer of sameness: "the human body loses its identity merely as a result of a change in the shape of some of its parts", while the mind "does not on that account become a different mind" (Synopsis, AT VII 14; CSM II 10). So sameness goes with the thinking, and belongs in D-I.

**S5 (an AI has no one body).** An AI can run as many instances at once, and on different machines at different times. If sameness rides on one body, an AI's "I" has no sameness at all. A psychological criterion in D-I, continuity of memory, would fit any thinker, which is what D-I was written to do.

**Conclusion of S.** D-I should say that "I" names the same thinker across the thoughts connected by memory, so that P1's demonstration is valid by definition.

## Thesis B: sameness does not belong in D-I

**B1 (the root stays as thin as the doubt).** A1 is certain because doubting it is a case of it. The doubt shows that the doubting goes on now, and no more: "I am, I exist, that is certain. But for how long? For as long as I am thinking" (AT VII 27). If D-I contained sameness over time, "I exist" would claim a lasting I, and the doubt does not show one. A1 would lose the one property that makes it the root (J1).

**B2 (memory cannot be the definition: Butler and Reid).** Butler: "one should really think it self-evident, that consciousness of personal identity presupposes, and therefore cannot constitute, personal identity; any more than knowledge, in any other case, can constitute truth, which it presupposes" (*Of Personal Identity*, 1736). To remember doing something is to remember *my* doing it, so the memory criterion is circular. Reid's brave officer adds that remembering is not transitive while identity is. The general remembers taking a standard as a young officer, and the officer remembered being flogged as a boy, but the general has forgotten the flogging (*Essays on the Intellectual Powers of Man*, 1785, Essay III, ch. 6). Put Locke's criterion into D-I and the definition becomes circular and inconsistent.

**B3 (Hume: thinking does not show a lasting I).** Hume looked for the self in experience and found only perceptions: the rest of mankind "are nothing but a bundle or collection of different perceptions, which succeed each other with an inconceivable rapidity, and are in a perpetual flux and movement" (*Treatise* 1.4.6.4). Then, in the Appendix, he confessed that on review "I find myself involv'd in such a labyrinth, that, I must confess, I neither know how to correct my former opinions, nor how to render them consistent." So the thinking that D-I refers to does not present a lasting I. A bundle criterion in D-I would install a view its own author could not make consistent.

**B4 (Parfit: identity can be indeterminate and depends on more than the thinker).** Parfit argues that what matters in survival is "Relation R: psychological connectedness and/or continuity, with the right kind of cause", which can hold in different degrees, and that personal identity is not what matters (*Reasons and Persons*, 1984, pp. 215–217, and ch. 12). His account of identity has a non-branching condition: the later person is the same only if no *other* person is also continuous with the earlier one. So whether a later thinker is the same can depend on whether a duplicate exists somewhere else. The definition of a word used in one thought cannot settle that. The AI case in S5, copies and instances, is exactly Parfit's branching case, and it needs a stated rule applied to evidence, not a definition of "I".

**B5 (Spinoza: sameness is the sameness of the body's organization).** Spinoza defines an individual by its union: bodies that "preserve among themselves a certain fixed relation ... together ... compose one body or individual" (E2, Definition after Lemma 3). An individual stays the same while its parts are replaced: "if, at the same time, an equal number of other bodies of the same nature take their place, the individual will preserve its nature as before, without any change in its actuality (forma)" (E2, Lemma 4; Latin *absque ulla ejus formae mutatione*). Lemmas 5–7 extend this to growth, change of direction, and motion as a whole. The mind is the idea of the body (E2P13), so the sameness of the mind follows the sameness of the body's organization. That is a fact about an organization, to be found out. It is not a meaning of a word.

**B6 (what can fail belongs where evidence reaches it).** Sameness can fail: the poet, death, a body that divides. A definition cannot be false (J2). Put sameness into D-I and those cases become either impossible or true by definition. Leave it to evidence and they can be judged.

**Conclusion of B.** D-I stays thin. Sameness is carried by A2: the I is one body, and the body's sameness is found on evidence.

**Scores on the criteria.**

- **J1.** S fails: a lasting I in D-I makes A1 claim more than the doubt shows. B passes.
- **J2.** S fails: a definition would carry what can be false. B passes.
- **J3.** S's memory criterion is circular (Butler) and intransitive (Reid). B's criterion, as amended, answers the poet, death, division and copies, and can fail.
- **J4.** B is faithful to E2, Lemmas 4–7, and E2P13, and it departs openly from E4P39, Scholium, by splitting "the same" from "how connected".
- **J5.** B, as amended, fits any whole, an AI included if it is one. S fits any thinker only by making A1 uncertain.
- **J6.** Both pass. "Connectedness" and "went on" describe; they do not name an aim.

## Verdict

**What falls.**

- Putting sameness into D-I.
- Locke's memory criterion as a definition.
- Descartes's thinking substance as the bearer of sameness.
- The phrase "one lasting body" in A2 and P1. It did not say what makes a body the same while its parts are replaced, and it said nothing about the change Spinoza saw in the poet.

**What survives.** Neither side as posed. Sameness is defined once, for all wholes, and it reaches the I only through what the I is found to be.

1. **D-I stays thin.** It names the doer of *this* thought, and it now says that sameness over time is defined elsewhere, for wholes.
2. **A new definition, D-whole, Definition 12 (the same whole).** A whole at a later time is *the same whole* as at an earlier time when its organization has gone on between the two times, with no time at which it performed none of its works as one, and when no other whole carries on that organization from the earlier time as fully as it does. Its members may be replaced meanwhile (after E2, Lemma 4). How far the later whole is *connected* to the earlier is a matter of degree: how far its works go on as they did. Memory is one of the works, so a loss of memory makes a whole less connected to its past. It does not make it a different whole, unless the work stops altogether, and then the thing is no longer a whole at all (D-whole, Definition 8).
3. **The I's sameness is then derived, not defined.** A2 identifies the I with this body, on evidence. P1, step 3, shows this body is a whole. Definition 12 says what it is for that whole to be the same across times. So the I of A2 is the I of A4. P1, step 6, states this, and P1's cap (A2, A3) already covers it.
4. **An honest departure.** For Spinoza the poet's body may have "undergone death" while it lived. For the book the poet is the same whole, much less connected. Spinoza left the question "undiscussed". The book settles the yes-or-no part by a test that can fail, and reports the rest as a degree.
5. **Parfit's caution, kept.** The book claims that sameness can be judged. It does not claim that sameness is what matters. Whether it matters, for responsibility or for what I have reason to care about, is a question for the book's later parts.
