**Definition 3 (work).** A *work* of a thing is an activity of that thing which (a) contributes to keeping the thing's organization going, and (b) is carried out by structures that the thing's organization itself produces or maintains.

"Contributes" is causal: without the activity, the organization would change or fail. No aim is implied. Condition (a) does not ask that the activity be indispensable; it may be one of several ways the organization is kept up. Mossio and colleagues' own example is the heart's pumping: it keeps the blood moving, which keeps the body's organization going, and the body in turn builds and maintains the heart.

Condition (b) is what separates a thing's work from a service done for it. A robot that returns to its charger when its battery runs low takes in energy, and this keeps it running. But people, not the robot, make and repair the parts that do it, so by (b) the activity is not a work of the robot's (A3, hard cases). An earlier wording of (b), "is itself kept going by that organization", could be read as any loop of cause and effect, and the robot passes that reading. The present wording follows the source (Scholium).

**Definition 4 (performs).** A thing *performs* a work when that activity is going on in it. Whether it is performed is a fact to be found. Whether it is *required* is a separate thesis (PR1) and adds nothing to the finding.

**Definition 5 (as one).** A thing performs a work *as one* when condition (b) holds at the thing's own level: the structures carrying out the activity are produced or maintained by the organization of the whole, not only by each member for its own sake, and not only by something outside the thing.

*The take-apart test.* Ask what would happen if the whole's organization were removed while each member was kept going by other means: cells kept alive in culture, people fed and sheltered. If the activity at the whole's scale would stop, the whole was performing it as one. If it would carry on unchanged in each member, the members were performing it separately. The test is a question about what depends on what. It is not a procedure: most of a body's cells die when the body is taken apart, and that death says nothing about which works the body performs as one.

*Example.* People avoid likely sources of infection through disgust. Disperse a society and each person still does this, so the society is not performing it; its members are, separately. Quarantine and organized public health stop when the institutions that run them are gone, so these are performed by the society as one.

**Definition 6 (the seven works).** The book uses seven works, each fixed by one test question.

| Work | Does the thing, as one, ... |
|---|---|
| **Boundary** | keep an edge that sorts, letting some things across and turning others back according to what they are? |
| **Control** | combine signals from its parts, and stored information, into changes that coordinate its parts? |
| **Energy** | take in energy or matter from outside and convert it into forms its other works use? |
| **Transport** | move materials between its parts? |
| **Signaling** | carry information between its parts? |
| **Defense** | detect agents that damage it and resist or remove them, beyond what the boundary keeps out? |
| **Memory** | store information and later use it in its other works? |

By this wording control uses signaling and memory: a thing that performs control as one is combining signals and stored information at its own level. P2 relies on this.

**Where the list comes from.** Six of the seven follow the critical subsystems that Miller (1978) finds in living systems at every level, grouped: boundary; decider (control); ingestor, converter and producer (energy); distributor (transport); channel and net, with transducers, decoder and encoder (signaling); memory. Miller counts more subsystems than these (the reproducer and the timer among them), and he has no separate defense subsystem. So the seven are a selection, and defense is the book's addition, drawn from cell-autonomous immunity, immune systems and social immunity (sources in A3 and A4). The selection is the book's own choice and is revisable if A3's test fails.

**Scholium (the organizational account).** Definitions 3–5 follow Mossio, Saborido & Moreno (2009), for whom a trait has a function if and only if "C1. T contributes to the maintenance of the organization O of S; C2. T is produced and maintained under some constraints exerted by O; C3. S is organizationally differentiated." The book keeps C1 and C2 as (a) and (b); "organization", as in Mossio and colleagues, means the structures and constraints that the parts impose on one another. C3, that the system has distinct parts making distinct contributions, is roughly what performing seven distinct works amounts to; the book does not test it separately. Mossio and colleagues use the account to ground the purposive and normative side of function talk; the book uses only the causal loop and takes no position on that further reading.

**Limit.** This is a definition. It fixes what the words mean and makes the later tests possible. It does not make any claim more likely.
