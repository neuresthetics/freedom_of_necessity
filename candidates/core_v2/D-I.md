**Definition 1 (I).** "I", used in a thought, names whatever is doing that thought: a thinker, if there is one distinct from the thinking, or else the thinking itself. *Thinking* here covers what Descartes lists: "a thing that doubts, understands, affirms, denies, is willing, is unwilling, and also imagines and has sensory perceptions" (Second Meditation, AT VII 28; CSM II 19).

**Explanation.**

- *Fixed by use.* The word names something only when it is used in a thought. D-I does not say that anything answers to it. That something does, whenever I doubt that I exist, is what A1 finds.
- *Lichtenberg allowed.* "Or else the thinking itself" takes the objection A1 already quotes: "It thinks, we should say, just as we say, it lightnings" (*Sudelbuch* K 76). D-I does not assume a thinker distinct from the thinking. If there is none, "I" names the thinking.
- *Sensory perception in the restricted sense.* Descartes counts perceiving as thinking only as *seeming* to perceive: "I certainly seem to see, to hear, and to be warmed. This cannot be false" (AT VII 29; CSM II 19). In the *Principles* he says that "I am walking, therefore I exist" is certain only if walking means the awareness of walking, not the movement of the body (*Principles* I.9, AT VIIIA 7). D-I follows him here. Only thinking counts, so the word names something whenever it is used, whatever else turns out false.

**What D-I leaves out.**

- *What the I is.* Body, soul or program: D-I does not say. It is a definition of a word, not of an essence. Spinoza counts as belonging to a thing's essence "that, which being given, the thing is necessarily given also, and, which being removed, the thing is necessarily removed also" (E2D2), and D-I claims no such thing. What the author's I is, A2 says, on evidence.
- *Whether the thinking is felt.* Descartes defines thought through immediate awareness (Second Replies, definition I, AT VII 160; CSM II 113). D-I does not, so it says nothing about feeling. This is what lets it fit an AI, whose awareness is undecided, and it keeps the hard problem where A2 leaves it: open.
- *Sameness over time.* D-I names the doer of *this* thought. It does not say that the doer of one thought is the doer of the next. A1 says the same ("For as long as I am thinking"). That the I of one entry is the I of another is carried by A2, on evidence, which identifies the doer with one lasting body.

**Scholium 1 (Spinoza).** The *Ethics* has no definition of "I". Part Two begins from the human mind: "The first element, which constitutes the actual being of the human mind, is the idea of some particular thing actually existing" (E2P11), and that thing is the body (E2P13). D-I starts thinner. Spinoza's content, the mind as the idea of the body, enters at A2, clause (b), as a declared reading. (Elwes 1883 wording, checked against `sources/spinoza/`.)

**Scholium 2 (an AI).** An AI that produces "I exist" uses the word. By D-I, the word names whatever is doing that thought, *if* producing the sentence is a case of doubting, affirming or the like. D-I does not decide whether it is. D-I takes Descartes's list but not his awareness clause, so whether doubting without awareness is still doubting is left open, and that is the AI question. So D-I fits an AI exactly as far as A1 does, and no further.

**Limit.** This is a definition. It says what the word names and makes no claim more likely. It carries no confidence.
