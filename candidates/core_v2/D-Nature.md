**Definition 13 (Nature).** *Nature taken as one* is everything that exists (D-exist), taken together, with nothing besides it. Following Spinoza, the book also calls it *God*: "the eternal and infinite Being, which we call God or Nature" (E4 Preface; Latin *Deum seu Naturam*).

**Explanation.**

- *Taken as one, not shown to be one.* "As one" here means only that everything is counted together and spoken of in the singular. That Nature is *really* one, a single substance and not a heap, is Spinoza's E1P14 ("Besides God no substance can be granted or conceived"), and it belongs to the declared reading below. An earlier wording, "conceived as one", could be heard either way.
- *A plurality, not a set.* "Everything that exists, taken together" speaks of many things at once. It does not posit a set that contains everything. In standard set theory there is no set of all sets, and nothing in the core needs one.
- *Nature is not finite.* A thing is finite when another thing of the same kind can limit it (D-whole, domain clause, after E1D2). There is nothing besides Nature, of any kind, to limit it.
- *Everything is in Nature.* Whatever exists is in Nature, by Definition 13. In this thin sense "in" means only *among everything that exists*. It is true of everything, so it sorts nothing.
- *In Nature is not member of.* Being a *member* of a whole (D-whole, Definition 9) is a relation between finite things: shared fate and function, judged by evidence and graded. Being *in Nature* holds of everything, whatever its grade. My cells are members of me, and I am a member of my society. All three are in Nature, and none of them is a member of Nature.
- *Why it matters for D-whole.* D-whole grades finite things only (its domain clause). So Nature taken as one is neither a whole nor a non-whole in the book's sense. D-whole's tests (what is sorted at the boundary, where energy comes from, what defense acts against) all assume that something else exists, and nothing exists besides Nature.

**Declared reading (not scored).** The book reads Definition 13 as Spinoza's God, "a being absolutely infinite—that is, a substance consisting in infinite attributes" (E1D6), and as one in his strong sense, "Besides God no substance can be granted or conceived" (E1P14), and reads "in Nature" as he reads "in God": "Whatsoever is, is in God, and without God nothing can be, or be conceived" (E1P15). Spinoza's "in" is stronger than Definition 13's. For him a mode "exists in, and is conceived through, something other than itself" (E1D5), so to be in God is also to be conceived through God. The core does not derive this, and no evidence in it could score it. Like A2's clause (b), it is declared and adds no confidence. Whether it should become an entry of its own is an open question (README, section 5).

**Scholium 1 (Spinoza's "whole" is wider than the book's).** In Letter 32, a thing is considered a whole in so far as no external cause acts on it. Blood with nothing beyond it "would then always have to be considered as a whole, not as a part" (Letter 32, to Oldenburg, 1665). On that concept Nature is the whole without qualification, and it is also "one individual, whose parts, that is, all bodies, vary in infinite ways, without any change in the individual as a whole" (E2, Lemma 7, Scholium). The book's "whole" is narrower: a finite thing that lasts by its own works while other things act on it. The two words should not be read as one. Spinoza's "part of Nature" is stated in E4P4: "It is impossible, that man should not be a part of Nature, or that he should be capable of undergoing no changes, save such as can be understood through his nature only as their adequate cause." So for him, to be a part of Nature is to undergo changes that one's own nature alone does not explain, that is, to be acted on by other things. That is the finite condition D-whole grades.

**Scholium 2 (not a whole of parts).** Taken as substance, Nature has no real parts: "Substance absolutely infinite is indivisible" (E1P13), and the parts of matter "are distinguished, not really, but modally" (E1P15, Scholium). This is a second reason, independent of finitude, why D-whole's language of members does not reach it.

**Scholium 3 (self-caused is not self-producing).** "Self-caused" (E1D1: "that of which the essence involves existence") and "produces itself" (D-whole, Definition 11: making and replacing worn components) share a word, not a concept. Nothing in the core infers one from the other.

**Scholium 4 (freedom).** Only Nature is free in Spinoza's sense: "That thing is called free, which exists solely by the necessity of its own nature, and of which the action is determined by itself alone" (E1D7), and "God is the sole free cause" (E1P17, Corollary 2). Every whole D-whole grades takes energy from other things, so each is, in E1D7's word, constrained. Keeping Nature out of D-whole keeps that line clear. E1D7's pair, *free* and *necessary* ("or rather constrained"), is the pair in the book's title.

**Limit.** This is a definition. It says what "Nature" means in the book, and it makes no claim more likely. That Nature is God in Spinoza's full sense is declared, not shown. The collision that produced this entry is [collision_nature.md](collision_nature.md).
