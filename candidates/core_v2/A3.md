**Axiom.** The seven works sort things the way self-production does. A thing that produces itself (D-whole, Definition 11) performs all seven as one; a thing that lasts without producing itself fails at least one.

**The independent test.** Self-production (D-whole, Definition 11): a thing's own activities make and replace the components that carry out those activities, the components of its edge included. It is defined without the seven works.

*Why a second test is needed.* If the seven works were checked only against themselves, "a stone is not a whole" would be true by definition and A3 would find nothing out. Self-production is defined without the seven works, so agreement between the two is a finding, and it can fail.

*Why self-production is not simply the definition of a whole.* It is yes-or-no, and hard to check directly for large, loose things like a society. The seven works can be checked one at a time, and graded. A3 is the claim that licenses using them: where both tests can be run, they agree.

*How far the two tests are apart.* D-work's condition (b) asks, for each work, that the structures carrying it out be produced or maintained by the thing's own organization. That brings the seven works closer to self-production than a bare list of activities would be, and A3 is to that extent less of a discovery. It is still not the same test. Definition 11 asks, once and for all, that a thing make and replace *all* the components that carry out its activities, the components of its edge included. Condition (b) is asked one work at a time, it may be met in part (grade 1), and it says nothing about the boundary unless the boundary work is checked on its own. So a thing could meet (b) on some works and fail Definition 11, or the reverse, and A3 can still fail.

**Anchors (should pass).** Free-living cells, nucleated body cells and multicellular organisms produce themselves, and each performs all seven works as one:

- *Boundary.* The cell membrane is a selectively permeable lipid bilayer that controls what enters and leaves (Singer & Nicolson 1972); skin and mucosal surfaces do this for the body.
- *Control.* Gene regulatory networks coordinate the cell (Davidson & Levine 2008); the nervous and endocrine systems coordinate the body.
- *Energy.* Cells convert nutrients into usable energy by glycolysis and respiration (Alberts et al. 2015); bodies digest, absorb and respire.
- *Transport.* Motor proteins carry cargo along the cytoskeleton (Vale 2003); blood and lymph move materials through the body.
- *Signaling.* Receptors and second messengers carry signals within and between cells (Alberts et al. 2015); nerves and hormones carry them through the body.
- *Defense.* Single cells defend themselves against pathogens (Randow, MacMicking & James 2013, "cell-autonomous immunity"); bacteria do so with restriction enzymes and CRISPR (Labrie, Samson & Moineau 2010); bodies have immune systems.
- *Memory.* Cells hold their identity through heritable marks such as DNA methylation (Bird 2002) and carry the genome across generations; bacterial CRISPR stores a record of past infections (Labrie, Samson & Moineau 2010); brains store memory through lasting synaptic change (Kandel 2001).

*A caution on the anchors.* Maturana and Varela called multicellular organisms "second-order" autopoietic systems, made of autopoietic cells, and left open whether they are autopoietic in the first-order sense (Maturana & Varela 1987). The book counts them as self-producing in the sense defined above, because they make and replace their own cells and tissues, skin included. That is a reading, stated here so it can be challenged.

**Negative controls (should fail at least one).** Each lasts, and none produces itself.

- *A stone* lasts for ages. Its surface does not sort what crosses it, and it takes in no energy. Boundary 0, energy 0.
- *A hurricane* can last for weeks. It draws heat from warm ocean water and moves air and moisture, so its energy and transport grades may be above zero. It organizes a flow of air and water but makes none of its own components; it has no edge that sorts, no memory and no defense. Degree 0.
- *An archive* can last for centuries and stores records, but the records are retrieved and used only by the institution that keeps it: take the staff away and the storage remains while the work of memory stops. It takes in no energy of its own and has no control of its own. Degree 0. It may be a member of an institution that is itself graded.

**Hard cases (where the test is most likely to break).**

- *A candle flame* keeps itself going: its heat melts and vaporizes the fuel that feeds it. Energy and transport may pass. But it consumes its components rather than making them, and it has no memory and no defense. Predicted degree 0, in agreement with self-production. This is the closest a non-producing thing comes to passing.
- *A virus particle* takes in no energy outside a cell. In a cell, its copying is carried out by the host's works, which by D-whole is a work done for it by something outside it. Predicted degree 0.
- *A mature red blood cell* has lost its nucleus and mitochondria, cannot make new protein and so cannot replace its damaged components, and lasts about 120 days (D'Alessandro et al. 2023). It does not produce itself. Its boundary and energy works pass, but with no genome and no protein synthesis it has no store of information that it later uses: predicted memory 0, degree 0. It is still a member of my body by D-whole, Definition 9, since it depends on my body's works and takes part in transport. So a thing can be a part without being a whole. Graders may disagree about its memory grade; if they do, that is test F3 at work.

- *A bacterium's transport* is the hard case in the other direction (F1). In small cells much of the movement of materials is diffusion. The cell keeps up the differences in concentration that diffusion runs down, so the movement arguably depends on the cell's organization; but if graders judge that diffusion is not transport performed as one, a self-producing thing gets a 0, and A3 fails as too strict until the transport question is rewritten.

- *A self-recharging robot* is the strongest case against A3 (F2). Grey Walter's robot tortoises were built to return to their hutch to recharge when their batteries ran low (Walter 1950), and present-day robot vacuums return to a dock. A robot of this kind can sense its surroundings, combine the signals in a controller, store a map and use it later, move charge between its components, steer clear of damage, and keep a casing that admits charge at its contacts while keeping out dust and water. If a work were any activity that helps keep a thing going and that the thing's organization keeps going, such a robot might pass all seven while producing none of its components. D-work, Definition 3(b), asks instead that the structures carrying out the activity be produced or maintained by the thing's own organization. People make and repair the robot's components, so every grade is 0. Predicted degree 0, in agreement with self-production. The prediction depends on that reading of (b); if (b) is loosened, A3 fails here.

The same reasoning grades today's AI systems: their hardware is built and maintained by people, so by D-work they perform none of the seven works as one and are not wholes in the book's sense. This says nothing about whether they think (D-I, Scholium 2) or whether anything is felt (A2).

These are predictions, not scored results.

**How A3 fails.**

- **F1 (too strict).** Some thing that produces itself gets grade 0 on one of the seven works.
- **F2 (too loose).** Some thing that does not produce itself gets grades above zero on all seven.
- **F3 (unusable).** Graders working apart from the same evidence often disagree about whether a grade is zero.

If F1 or F2 happens, the list or its test questions are revised, the revision is recorded, and everything that uses D-work and D-whole is rescored. A case is never saved by redescribing it after its grade is known.

**Limits.**

- The anchors are the kind of system Miller drew his subsystems from, so the anchors passing is expected and is weak evidence. The negative controls and the hard cases carry the test.
- A3 claims that the seven works are *performed* by self-producing things and absent from the controls. Whether they are *required* is PR1's thesis and adds no confidence here.
- A3 says nothing about societies. Whether a society performs the seven works as one is A4, on its own evidence.
