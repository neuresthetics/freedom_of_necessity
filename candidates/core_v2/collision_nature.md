# Collision: is Nature, taken as one, a whole?

**Status.** A candidate collision, written 2026-09-29 PT at the author's request ("collide that"). It judges between two readings. It scores nothing and changes nothing in `entries/`. Its result is the proposed entry [D-Nature](D-Nature.md), a domain clause added to [D-whole](D-whole.md), and a rewritten P1, Scholium 3.

**The question.** P1, Scholium 3, said that by D-whole, "nature as a whole gets grade 0 on boundary and energy", so it "is not a whole in this book's sense". Is that right? Or is Nature taken as one (Spinoza's *Deus seu Natura*) a whole, one that needs its own entry?

**Terms.** *Whole*, *grade*, *member* and *produces itself* are as in D-whole, Definitions 7–11. *Nature taken as one* means everything that exists, conceived as one, with nothing besides it. That is Spinoza's "eternal and infinite Being, which we call God or Nature" (E4 Preface). In the *Ethics* the Latin is *Deum seu Naturam* (E4 Preface) and *Dei seu Naturae* (E4P4, Demonstration). *Deus sive Natura* is the form usually quoted; the sense is the same.

**Rules of the collision.** Each side is stated at its strongest. Each thesis names what it stands on. The two are then judged side by side on five criteria:

- **J1.** Fidelity to Spinoza's texts.
- **J2.** Consistency with the core's own definitions.
- **J3.** Whether the resulting classification can fail. This is the standard the rebuild applied to D-whole: a definition that no case can fail rules nothing out.
- **J4.** No aiming words, and no "inside/outside" for the felt side.
- **J5.** What the book needs. Its subtitle begins with God.

Spinoza is quoted in the Elwes 1883 wording, checked against `sources/spinoza/ethics_elwes_1883.txt` and the Latin in `sources/spinoza/ethica_latin.txt`. The standard text is Kisner (Cambridge), cited by part and proposition, with pages pending the author's copy. The letters are quoted in Elwes's translation of the correspondence and numbered in the modern way, with Elwes's number in brackets.

## Thesis W: Nature taken as one is a whole, and needs its own entry

**W1 (Spinoza's own concept of a whole).** In Letter 32 [Elwes XV], to Oldenburg, 20 November 1665, Spinoza imagines a worm living in the blood. He says that if there were "no causes external to the blood ... nor any space beyond the blood, nor any bodies whereto the particles of blood could communicate their motion", then "the blood would then always have to be considered as a whole, not as a part". Because other causes do act on it, "the blood comes to be regarded as a part, not as a whole." So for Spinoza, a thing counts as a part because of external causes, and as a whole in so far as there are none. Besides God no substance can be granted or conceived (E1P14), whatever is, is in God (E1P15), and God is "the indwelling and not the transient cause of all things" (E1P18). So nothing external acts on Nature, and by Spinoza's own concept Nature is the one thing that is a whole without qualification. D-whole makes being a whole depend on something else, an edge to sort at and a source to take energy from. That reverses Spinoza.

**W2 (the infinite individual).** Spinoza builds individuals out of individuals and goes on: "We may easily proceed thus to infinity, and conceive the whole of nature as one individual, whose parts, that is, all bodies, vary in infinite ways, without any change in the individual as a whole" (E2, Lemma 7, Scholium; Latin *totam naturam unum esse Individuum*). In Letter 64, to Schuller, 29 July 1675, he gives as his example of a mediate infinite mode "the face of the whole universe, which, although it varies in infinite ways, yet remains always the same" (*facies totius Universi, quae quamvis infinitis modis variet, manet tamen semper eadem*), and refers the reader to that Scholium. So Spinoza calls Nature an individual with parts, one that stays the same while its parts change. That is what the core looks for in a body that replaces its cells.

**W3 (the zero is false).** By D-whole, Definition 7, grade 0 means that X "does not perform the work as one". That happens when the activity is absent at X's level, when only X's members do it, or when something outside X does it for X. Nature's zero on boundary and energy is none of these failures. The test question cannot be put to it, because there is nothing to sort and nothing to take energy from. A zero that anything without an outside would get, whatever it was like, measures the test and not the thing. PR1's requirements are conditional ("*if* a thing is to last as one"), and Nature is not in the condition. So its lack of those works shows no lack in it.

**W4 (the self-caused).** "By that which is self-caused, I mean that of which the essence involves existence" (E1D1), and "Existence belongs to the nature of substances" (E1P7). Everything that follows from the necessity of God's nature, *natura naturata*, follows from Nature viewed as active, *natura naturans* (E1P29, Scholium), and God remains its cause (E1P18). If anything produces itself, Nature does. D-whole, Definition 11, looks like a finite copy of that.

**W5 (the book's need).** Spinoza says outright: "It is impossible, that man should not be a part of Nature" (E4P4). P1 says that I am a part at the next level up. Followed upward, the levels arrive at Nature, and the core has to say what they arrive at. A book titled *God, Brain, and the Order of the Mind* that leaves God out of its core leaves the first word of its subtitle without an entry.

**Conclusion of W.** Nature taken as one is a whole, in fact the whole. D-whole should be widened so that it counts, and Nature should get its own entry.

## Thesis N: Nature taken as one is not a whole in D-whole's sense, and calls for different handling

**N1 (no parts).** "No attribute of substance can be conceived from which it would follow that substance can be divided" (E1P12). "Substance absolutely infinite is indivisible" (E1P13), and so "no substance, and consequently no extended substance, in so far as it is substance, is divisible" (E1P13, Corollary). E1P15, Scholium, draws the consequence for quantity: "infinite quantity is not measurable, and cannot be composed of finite parts". Parts of matter "are distinguished, not really, but modally" (*modaliter tantum distinguuntur, non autem realiter*). Water can be divided "in so far as it is water", but "in so far as it is extended substance" it is "neither separated nor divisible". D-whole's wholes are wholes of members that are really distinct things: a red blood cell dies while the body goes on, and I can leave a society (P1, Scholium 2). Nature as substance has no members of that kind. Calling it a whole of parts is the very mistake E1P15, Scholium, refutes.

**N2 (no outside, so no contrast).** Every test in D-work assumes that something else exists. The boundary sorts what crosses. Energy is taken in from somewhere. Defense acts against agents. The take-apart test takes the thing apart and leaves its members alive, but substance cannot be taken apart (E1P13). Put to Nature, each test question has an answer fixed in advance by the definition of Nature, so every grade would be 0 because the test cannot be put, or 2 by stipulation. A measure whose result is settled by the definition of the case gives no information about it (J3). This is the fault the rebuild removed from the old D-whole.

**N3 (lasting by works, and being by necessity).** D-whole's wholes last because their works keep them going (D-work, Definition 3), and the same works are what can fail. Nature's constancy ("without any change in the individual as a whole"; "always remains the same") is not the result of works holding off wear. The eternal and infinite Being "acts by the same necessity as that whereby it exists" (E4 Preface), and "nature has no particular goal in view" (E1, Appendix). To describe Nature by works invites saying that things work *for* it, and that is the aiming language E1, Appendix, rejects (J4).

**N4 (the one free thing).** "That thing is called free, which exists solely by the necessity of its own nature, and of which the action is determined by itself alone" (E1D7), and "God is the sole free cause" (E1P17, Corollary 2). Every finite thing is "conditioned for existence and action by a cause other than itself" (E1P28). Every whole in D-whole's sense takes its energy from other things, so each is, in E1D7's word, constrained. Put Nature into the same class and the one free cause is filed with the constrained things. That blurs the distinction E1D7 draws. E1D7's pair, *free* and *necessary*, is the pair in the book's title.

**N5 (Letter 32 cuts both ways).** In the same letter Spinoza marks the difference W1 passes over. "As the nature of the universe is not limited, like the nature of blood, but is absolutely infinite, its parts are by this nature of infinite power infinitely modified". Then: "But, in respect to substance, I conceive that each part has a more close union with its whole", since "each part belongs to the nature of substance, and, without it, can neither be nor be conceived". That is not the blood's relation to its particles. It is the relation of E1P15: "Whatsoever is, is in God, and without God nothing can be, or be conceived". The letter also makes whole and part a matter of how far things agree: where they do not, each "forms, in our mind, a separate idea". And it admits that to know how the parts of nature are really associated, "we should have to know the whole of nature and its several parts".

**N6 (the infinite individual is modal and not demonstrated).** E2, Lemma 7, Scholium, speaks of bodies, which are modes, and only "conceive[s]" the step to infinity. Spinoza declines to demonstrate it, since he is not "writing a special treatise on body". Letter 64 files the face of the whole universe under the mediate infinite modes: what follows from an attribute of God "in so far as it is modified by a modification, which exists necessarily and as infinite", and which "must also exist necessarily and as infinite" (E1P22). An infinite mode is not finite, so it is not the kind of thing D-whole was built to grade.

**Conclusion of N.** Nature taken as one is not a whole in D-whole's sense. Grading it at all is a category mistake. It belongs in a different entry, with a different relation.

**Scores on the criteria.**

- **J1 (fidelity).** N is closer to E1P12–15. W is closer to the letter of Letter 32 and E2, Lemma 7, Scholium, but N5 shows the same letter supports N.
- **J2 (consistency).** W's widened D-whole would need a grade for "the test cannot be put", which D-whole does not have.
- **J3 (can it fail).** W fails: a Nature graded as a whole can never be graded otherwise. N passes, since the classification of finite things keeps its zero.
- **J4 (no aims, no inside).** N passes. W's "works" of Nature invite aims.
- **J5 (the book's need).** W wins, and the proposal below meets it.

## Verdict

**What falls.** The claim that Nature taken as one is a whole in D-whole's sense. So does the old P1, Scholium 3, which gave Nature grade 0. Nature is not graded at all.

**What survives.**

1. **A domain.** D-whole grades finite things only: things that another thing of the same kind can limit (E1D2). Nature taken as one is not finite, so D-whole neither includes nor excludes it. This is added to D-whole as a domain clause.
2. **An entry for Nature.** A definition, [D-Nature](D-Nature.md), placed last among the definitions, after D-whole, because it uses D-whole's word *finite*.
3. **Two relations, kept apart.** *Member of* (D-whole, Definition 9: shared fate and function, graded) holds between finite things. *In Nature* holds between anything that exists and Nature. It is true of everything that exists, so it grades nothing. P1's levels of membership are levels among finite things, and none of them is Nature. Every level, and everything at every level, is in Nature.
4. **An honest note on words.** The book's "whole" is narrower than Spinoza's. For Spinoza, Nature is most of all a whole (Letter 32) and an individual (E2, Lemma 7, Scholium). For the book, "whole" names finite things that last by their works. D-Nature says so in its scholium.

**What stays open.** Whether the book also wants Spinoza's stronger claim as a claim of its own: that everything is in Nature in his sense of *in* (E1D5, E1P15, conceived through it and not only included in it), and that Nature is God as E1D6 defines God. Neither is self-evident, and neither carries evidence that could be scored. HOW_THIS_BOOK_IS_BUILT.md has no kind of entry for such a claim. D-Nature therefore carries the identification with God as a *declared reading*, like A2's clause (b). It is stated, not scored, and adds no confidence. See README, open questions.

- **Order.** Definitions first: D-I, D-exist, D-work, D-whole, **D-Nature**; then A1 and the rest (README, section 1). D-whole's domain clause defines *finite* (after E1D2); D-Nature uses it.
