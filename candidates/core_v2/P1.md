**Proposition 1.** I am a whole at one level and a part at the next.

**Demonstration.**

1. I am a human body made of cells (A2), so I am a multicellular organism.
2. A multicellular organism produces itself (read as in A3's caution on the anchors), so it performs all seven works as one (A3).
3. So each of my seven grades is above zero, my degree is above zero, and I am a whole (by 2 and D-whole, Definition 8).
4. My cells are members of me: each depends on my works (my transport brings it energy, my defense protects it) and takes part in at least one of them (A2, A3 anchors; D-whole, Definition 9). So I am a whole at the level whose members are cells.
5. The society I live in performs each of the seven works as one, at least in part, and I am one of its members (A4).
6. The I that A4 counts as a member is the I of step 1. By D-I, "I" names the doer of each thought, and A2 identifies that doer with this one lasting body. A4's membership evidence (food, public health, work) is about this same body, which is why A4 cites A2. So one and the same I is the whole of step 3 and the member of step 5.
7. So that society is a whole (by 5 and D-whole, Definition 8), and it is at the next level up from me (D-whole, Definition 10).
8. By 4, 6 and 7, I am a whole at one level and a part at the next. ∎

**Cap.** P1 cites A2, A3 and A4, so it is no stronger than the weakest of them (METHOD.md, rule 1). Its first half, *I am a whole*, rests only on A2 and A3, so it can be no weaker than its second half, which also needs A4.

**Scholium 1 (the levels between).** In Miller's (1978) hierarchy, persons make up groups, groups make up organizations, and organizations make up societies. By D-whole, Definition 10, the society is still at the next level up from me, since I am its member directly: I depend on its works and take part in them (A4). The groups I belong to are also at the next level up from me, and are themselves members of the society. Levels here are relative, not rungs on one ladder. P1 claims the direct membership; it does not deny the others.

**Scholium 2 (not a somatic cell).** Being a member is not being like a cell in a body in every respect. My body's cells cannot leave it, and their interests are almost entirely aligned with it. I can leave a society, and my interests can conflict with it (Queller & Strassmann 2009). D-whole records that conflict as a lower grade for the society, not as a fact about me.

**Scholium 3 (Spinoza, and where the levels stop).** Spinoza builds bodies the same way: "The human body is composed of a number of individual parts, of diverse nature, each one of which is in itself extremely complex" (E2, Postulate 1). Individuals can be composed of individuals, level on level, up to "the whole of nature as one individual" (E2, Lemma 7, Scholium). P1 goes one level up and no further. The levels of membership do not reach Nature. D-whole grades finite things only, and Nature taken as one is not finite (D-Nature), so it is neither a whole nor a non-whole in the book's sense, and nothing is a member of it. Every level, and everything at every level, is *in* Nature (D-Nature), and that relation is not membership. An earlier draft said that Nature gets grade 0 on boundary and energy. That was a false zero: the tests cannot be put to something with nothing besides it ([collision_nature.md](collision_nature.md)). Spinoza's own "whole" is wider than the book's. For him Nature is the whole without qualification (Letter 32), and the book does not dispute that. (Elwes 1883 wording, checked against `sources/spinoza/`.)
