# Collision: a full hand run over the twelve core v2 entries

**What this is.** Each core v2 entry was put through the method of the three earlier collisions ([collision_nature.md](../collision_nature.md), [collision_sameness.md](../collision_sameness.md), [collision_order.md](../collision_order.md)), with the patches agreed since:

1. The strongest case **for** the entry (F) and the strongest case **against** it (X), each set out with its premises stated.
2. **Shared floor.** The premises both sides need. If the entry fails on a premise both sides accept, that is a break, not a draw.
3. **Equivocation split.** Where the crux is one word covering two claims, the word is split and each claim is collided on its own.
4. **Judgment**, side by side. What survives is kept. More than one reading may survive.
5. **Verdict**: holds, holds with revision, splits, or breaks; the surviving wording; a one-line reason.

No numbers are given. Scores stay invalid until the collider is run on the full tower. Run by hand, 30 September 2026 PT.

Where an entry's wording changed, the old wording is quoted here and the new wording is in the entry's own file. Entries are taken in page order (README, section 1).

## 1. D-I (Definition 1, "I")

**Old wording.** "'I', used in a thought, names whatever is doing that thought: a thinker, if there is one distinct from the thinking, or else the thinking itself."

**For (F).**

- F1. The core's chains use "I", and they need the word to name something whenever it is used.
- F2. Of any thought, either there is a thinker distinct from the thinking, or there is not (Lichtenberg's "it thinks", *Sudelbuch* K 76, allows the second).
- F3. A definition that names the thinker in the first case, and the thinking in the second, names something whenever thinking goes on.
- *Proposition.* So D-I names something whenever "I" is used in a thought, and it does so without deciding between Descartes and Lichtenberg.

**Against (X).**

- X1. "Doing" covers two claims. It can mean that an agent performs an act, or only that an act occurs.
- X2. In the agent sense, "the thinking itself" is doing the thinking: an occurrence performing itself. That is a muddle.
- X3. In the occurrence sense, the first clause adds nothing about agents, and "doing" is idle.
- X4. Anscombe: "'I' is neither a name nor another kind of expression whose logical role is to make a reference, at all" ("The First Person", 1975, p. 60). So no definition of what "I" names can be correct.
- *Proposition.* So D-I is either muddled (X2) or about a word that names nothing (X4).

**Shared floor.** Both sides accept F1, as a requirement on the book's own word, and F2, since X uses Lichtenberg's case as well. Both treat D-I as a stipulation for this book.

**Split.** "Doing" → (a) an agent performs it; (b) it occurs.

- On (a), the old second clause fails: the thinking is not its own agent (X2 wins).
- On (b), the old wording holds, but it says less than it seems to.
- What survives both: drop "doing". Name the *thinker* in the first case, and the *thinking* in the second. Nothing is said to do itself.

**Anscombe.** X4 does not fall on the shared floor. The floor is F1, a requirement on the book's word, and a stipulation can meet it whatever ordinary "I" does. If Anscombe is right about ordinary use, D-I's "I" is a term of art. A1 survives either way, because its certainty rests on the thinking going on (section 3).

**Verdict: holds with revision.**

- *Surviving wording.* "'I', used in a thought, names the thinker of that thought, if there is a thinker distinct from the thinking, and otherwise the thinking itself."
- *Reason.* "Doing" covered an agent and an occurrence, and the thinking does not do itself. Anscombe's objection is now answered in a new Scholium 3, which marks the word as a term of art.

## 2. D-exist (Definition 2, "exist", "last")

**Old wording.** "A thing *exists* at a time when, at that time, it is going on (if it is an activity) or is present (if it is not). A thing *lasts* when it goes on existing through a stretch of time."

**For (F).**

- F1. Without a definition, A1's "exist" could be read in Spinoza's strongest sense, existence by the necessity of one's own nature (E1D1, E1P7), which the doubt does not show.
- F2. Doubting is an activity, and an activity that is going on now exists now in the thinnest sense.
- F3. A definition that ties existence to a time licenses only what the doubt shows.
- *Proposition.* So D-exist keeps A1 to exactly what doubting shows.

**Against (X).**

- X1. "Present" covers two claims: *present at a time*, which is just existing at that time (circular), or *present to someone*, at hand (false, since things exist unperceived). On either reading the second clause defines nothing.
- X2. The old wording defined "exists" as "exists at a time". But "God, and all the attributes of God, are eternal" (E1P19), and eternal existence "cannot be explained by means of continuance or time" (E1D8, Explanation). D-Nature's declared reading identifies Nature with God, and D-Nature defines Nature through D-exist. So the old wording either leaves God out of "everything that exists" or contradicts its own claim to leave eternity open.
- X3. Kant: "'Being' is obviously not a real predicate; that is, it is not a concept of something which could be added to the concept of a thing" (*Critique of Pure Reason*, A598/B626). So a definition of existence adds nothing.
- *Proposition.* So the second clause of D-exist is empty, and its time clause clashes with the book's declared reading of Nature.

**Shared floor.** Both sides accept F1 and F2. Both accept that D-Nature's identification of Nature with God is declared in the book, and that D-exist says it leaves "for a time or eternally" open, which is D-exist's own text.

**The break.** X2 succeeds on the shared floor. A definition that makes all existence existence at a time does not leave eternity open, though D-exist says it does. That is a real break in the old wording, not a draw.

**Split.** "Exist" → (a) an activity going on at a time; (b) existence for anything that is not an activity.

- (a) survives every objection. It is all that A1 uses (F2), and it is not circular: "going on" is said of activities and is not a synonym of "exists".
- (b) was defined only by "present", which X1 shows to be circular or false. It is withdrawn, and "exists" is taken as understood for things that are not activities.
- X3 does not break the survivor. D-exist adds no property to a thing; it fixes what A1's word claims and blocks the strong reading. Kant's point supports leaving (b) undefined.
- Once existence is defined only for activities, X2 lapses: nothing in D-exist now says that whatever exists, exists at a time.

**Verdict: splits.** The activity clause and the lasting clause survive; the "present" clause breaks.

- *Surviving wording.* "An activity *exists* at a time when it is going on at that time. For anything that is not an activity, 'exists' is taken as understood, and D-exist does not define it. A thing *lasts* when it goes on existing through a stretch of time."
- *Reason.* A1 needs only the activity clause. "Present" defined by a synonym. The old time clause did not leave eternity open, though it said it did. This settles README open question 16.

## 3. A1 ("I exist")

**Old gloss (the axiom is unchanged).** "In defined terms: whatever is doing this thinking is going on, or present, now."

**For (F).**

- F1. Doubting is a case of thinking (D-I, after Descartes's list).
- F2. While I doubt that I exist, this doubting is going on.
- F3. An activity that is going on exists then (D-exist, revised).
- F4. An activity cannot go on while what does it, if anything does, fails to exist.
- F5. "I" names the thinker of this thought, if there is a thinker distinct from it, and otherwise the thinking itself (D-I, revised).
- *Proposition.* On the second reading of "I", what "I" names exists by F2 and F3. On the first, it exists by F2, F3 and F4. So, on either reading, I exist.

**Against (X).**

- X1. Lichtenberg: only "it thinks" is warranted. "I" adds a subject the doubt does not show.
- X2. Hume reports that when he looks for a self he finds only particular perceptions (*Treatise* 1.4.6). So there is no I to exist.
- X3. Anscombe (as in D-I): "I" does not refer, so "I exist" says nothing about anything.
- X4. The old gloss used "present", which D-exist now withdraws, and joined the two readings of "I" with one verb.
- *Proposition.* So A1 either claims more than the doubt shows, or claims nothing.

**Shared floor.** Both sides accept F1 and F2. No one can object without doubting or thinking, and Lichtenberg's and Hume's own reports are reports of thinking going on.

**Split.** "I" → (a) a thinker distinct from the thinking; (b) the thinking.

- X1 and X2 are objections to (a) alone. On (b), A1 claims only what they grant: this thinking is going on. A1 holds on (b) on the shared floor alone.
- On (a), A1 adds only F4, which no side denies: there is no doing without a doer, if there is a doer.
- "Exist" → (a) going on now; (b) existing by one's own nature. D-exist blocks (b). A1 claims (a).
- X3 is answered as in D-I: the book's "I" is a term of art, and A1's certainty does not rest on how "I" refers.
- X4 is right about the gloss, and the gloss is rewritten.

**Verdict: holds.** The statement and its root standing are unchanged; the gloss was rewritten.

- *Surviving wording.* "I exist." In defined terms: "this thinking is going on now, and so its thinker, if it has one distinct from it, exists now."
- *Reason.* It holds on both readings of "I", using only what every objector grants by objecting.

## 4. A2 (a human body, and its mind)

**Statement (unchanged, as the author accepted it).** "I am a human body made of cells, and my mind is that same body's organization described in the first person."

**Old scored reading.** "(a) Empirical: the one who exists (A1) is a living human body made of cells, and its mental abilities vary with that body's organization."

**For (F).**

- F1. Living bodies are made of cells, and cells come from cells (cell theory).
- F2. This thinking goes with this body: closing these eyes ends my seeing, and so on.
- F3. Specific damage removes specific abilities (Broca 1861; Scoville & Milner 1957).
- F4. One thing described two ways predicts that body and mind vary together, and posits no second thing (economy; E2P7 Scholium, E3P2 Scholium).
- *Proposition.* So I am this body, and my mind is its organization described in the first person.

**Against (X).**

- X1. The constitution view: a person is constituted by a human body without being identical with it, as a statue is constituted by its bronze (Baker 2000). F1–F3 fit it exactly.
- X2. The transplant case: if this brain were kept alive in another body, many would say I go with the brain. If so, I am not identical with the whole body. Animalism answers the case (Olson 1997), but the answer is a philosophical argument, not something F1–F3 decide.
- X3. "Made of cells" is too strong: a body is also made of what cells make, such as bone mineral and the extracellular matrix (Alberts et al. 2015).
- *Proposition.* So the evidence does not show that I am identical with this body, and "made of cells" overclaims.

**Shared floor.** Both sides accept F1–F3 and X3's facts. Both accept that evidence equally consistent with two views cannot score one of them. That is the rule A2 already applies to its clause (b).

**Split.** "Am" → (a1) *made of and goes with*: I am made of this body, and this thinking goes on with it and varies with it; (a2) *strict identity*: I am nothing other than this body.

- (a1) survives every objection. X1 and X2 both grant it.
- (a2) fails on the shared floor as a *scored* claim. The evidence fits constitution and identity alike, so by A2's own rule identity cannot be scored. It survives as a *reading*, and it belongs with clause (b), where the book's choice for one thing described two ways already lives.
- X3 is correct as a matter of fact. "Made of cells" stays in the statement, as short for cells and what cells make, and the text now says so.

**Verdict: splits.**

- *Survivor 1 (scored).* "I am made of a human body, of cells and what cells make, and this thinking goes on with that body and varies with its organization."
- *Survivor 2 (declared, unscored).* "I am this body, and my mind is its organization described in the first person."
- *Wording.* The statement is kept as the author accepted it. The entry now says what "am" is scored as (a new paragraph, "What 'am' claims"), with Baker and Olson cited, and the cell bullet adds what cells make.
- *Reason.* The evidence shows what I am made of and what I go with, not identity. Whether the statement itself should say "made of" is a new open question.

## 5. D-work (Definitions 3–6, work and the seven works)

**Old wording of Definition 3.** "A *work* of a thing is an activity of that thing which (a) contributes to keeping the thing's organization going, and (b) is itself kept going by that organization."

**Old take-apart test (Definition 5).** "Disperse the whole while leaving its members alive. If the activity at the whole's scale stops, the whole was performing it as one."

**For (F).**

- F1. Function can be defined without aims, as a loop: the activity helps keep the organization up, and the organization keeps the activity up (Mossio, Saborido & Moreno 2009).
- F2. D-work follows their C1 and C2 as (a) and (b), as its Scholium says.
- F3. "As one" needs a test that separates a whole's work from its members' separate activity.
- *Proposition.* So D-work defines work without aims, and gives "as one" a test.

**Against (X).**

- X1. A robot that returns to its charger when its battery runs low (Grey Walter's tortoises were built to do this, Walter 1950; robot vacuums do it today) has an activity that helps keep it running (a) and is kept going by its own control loop (b, read loosely). It can have something like all seven activities, its casing counting as an edge that admits charge at the contacts and keeps out dust and water. It produces none of its parts. So D-work, read loosely, makes it a candidate whole, and A3 fails (F2 of A3).
- X2. Mossio's C2 reads: "T is produced and maintained under some constraints exerted by O." The old (b) is looser than its own source.
- X3. The take-apart test cannot be run on a body. Disperse a body's cells and most of them die, so the test gives no answer, or a wrong one.
- *Proposition.* So D-work is too loose, and its test cannot be used.

**Shared floor.** Both sides accept F1, F2 and F3: D-work says it follows C2, and neither side wants aims.

**The break.** On the shared floor (F2), the old (b) does not say what C2 says. The loose reading of (b) is not the one D-work claims to take, and on it X1 succeeds.

**Split.** "Kept going by that organization" → (i) caught in any loop of cause and effect with it; (ii) carried out by structures that the organization itself produces or maintains (C2).

- (i) breaks on X1. The robot's activity is caught in a loop with its organization.
- (ii) survives. People, not the robot, make and repair the robot's parts. The heart passes: the body builds and maintains it.

**Split.** "Take apart" → (i) a procedure actually carried out; (ii) a question about what depends on what: would the activity at the whole's scale stop if the whole's organization were removed while the members were kept going by other means?

- (i) breaks on X3.
- (ii) survives. Cells kept alive in culture show what a cell does without the body; organized public health shows what a society does that its members do not.

**Verdict: holds with revision.**

- *Surviving wording.* Definition 3: "(b) is carried out by structures that the thing's organization itself produces or maintains." Definition 5: as one when "the structures carrying out the activity are produced or maintained by the organization of the whole"; the take-apart test is restated as a question about dependence, with a note that cells dying when a body is taken apart says nothing.
- *Reason.* The revision says what its stated source says, and the loose reading lets a charging robot in. The cost falls on A3 (section 8).

## 6. D-whole (Definitions 7–12)

**Old wording of Definition 9.** "A thing is a *member* (a part) of a whole W when (a) its persistence depends on at least one of W's works, and (b) it takes part in at least one of them."

**For (F).**

- F1. A measure of wholeness needs a true zero, or it rules nothing out (Definitions 7 and 8).
- F2. Seven different works do not make up for one another, so the lowest grade is the degree, reported with the profile.
- F3. Membership is judged by function, not origin (mitochondria, the microbiota).
- F4. Sameness is defined once, for wholes, and can fail (Definition 12).
- *Proposition.* So D-whole can sort things, and can say what belongs to them and when they are the same.

**Against (X).**

- X1. Membership is too cheap. A visitor depends on a society's works and takes part in them, and so does a dog in a household. And the old "when" read as timeless: someone who left long ago could still count.
- X2. The lowest grade throws information away.
- X3. Definition 12's third condition makes sameness depend on what happens elsewhere. Whether I am the same whole depends on whether some other whole carries my organization on as fully. This is a standing objection to every non-branching condition.
- X4. "Whole" covers the book's narrow sense and Spinoza's wide one (Letter 32; E2, Lemma 7, Scholium).
- *Proposition.* So D-whole's member is too wide, its degree too coarse, and its sameness too extrinsic.

**Shared floor.** Both sides accept F3: the objection in X1 uses functional tests too. Both accept that membership changes over time, since people join and leave.

**Split.** "Member" → (a) a member while the conditions hold; (b) a member once and for all.

- (b) breaks on the shared floor: membership changes.
- (a) survives. The visitor and the dog are members while they meet both conditions. That is accepted, and the entry now says so. D-whole sorts what takes part in a whole's working, not what belongs to it by kind, origin or right.

**The rest.**

- X2 is answered by the text already: the profile is always reported beside the degree, so nothing is thrown away.
- X3 is a cost that is accepted, as Parfit accepts it, and D-whole already says that it does not claim sameness is what matters. It does not fall on the shared floor. A dividing cell gives both sides a case where neither daughter is the same cell.
- X4 was split in the Nature collision (D-Nature, Scholium 1).

**Verdict: holds with revision.**

- *Surviving wording.* Definition 9: "A thing is a member (a part) of a whole W *while* (a) ... and (b) ...", followed by a new paragraph on membership held for a time, with the visitor and the dog as accepted consequences.
- *Reason.* Membership comes and goes, and functional membership is cheap by design.

## 7. D-Nature (Definition 13)

**Old wording.** "*Nature taken as one* is everything that exists (D-exist), conceived as one, with nothing besides it."

**For (F).**

- F1. The book needs a name for everything that exists, so that D-whole's domain can be marked (only finite things are graded).
- F2. Nothing besides everything limits it, so it is not finite (E1D2).
- F3. The identification with God is declared, not scored.
- *Proposition.* So D-Nature marks the edge of D-whole without claiming Spinoza's metaphysics.

**Against (X).**

- X1. "Everything that exists, conceived as one" is either the set of everything, which standard set theory does not allow (there is no set of all sets), or a mere heap, in which case "as one" is idle.
- X2. "Conceived as one" can be heard as "is one", which is Spinoza's monism: "Besides God no substance can be granted or conceived" (E1P14). That is part of what the definition says it leaves to the declared reading.
- X3. D-Nature defines through D-exist. On D-exist's old wording, what exists eternally was left out (section 2).
- *Proposition.* So D-Nature either posits a set it cannot have or carries monism unannounced.

**Shared floor.** Both sides accept F1 and F2, and accept that whatever is declared adds no confidence (F3).

**Split.** "One" → (i) counted as one: everything spoken of together, in the singular; (ii) really one: one substance.

- (i) survives, and it needs no set. "Everything that exists, taken together" refers to many things at once. It does not name one set that contains them.
- (ii) fails as part of the definition, on the shared floor (F3): it is a claim, and Spinoza argues for it (E1P14). It survives as part of the declared reading, beside God.
- X3 lapses with D-exist's revision.

**Verdict: holds with revision.**

- *Surviving wording.* "*Nature taken as one* is everything that exists (D-exist), taken together, with nothing besides it." The entry adds that "as one" means counted as one, and that "really one" (E1P14) is declared.
- *Reason.* The definition marks a domain; that Nature is one substance is a claim, and is declared as one.

## 8. A3 (the seven works sort things the way self-production does)

**For (F).**

- F1. Self-production (Definition 11) is defined without the seven works.
- F2. The anchors (cells, organisms) pass all seven; the controls (stone, hurricane, archive) fail at least one; the hard cases (flame, virus, red blood cell) are predicted correctly.
- F3. Agreement between two separately defined tests is a finding, and can fail (F1–F3 of A3).
- *Proposition.* So the seven works sort things the way self-production does.

**Against (X): a dilemma.**

- X1. If D-work's (b) is read loosely, a self-recharging robot is predicted to pass all seven works without producing its parts. That is A3's own failure F2.
- X2. If (b) is read strictly, a work's structures have to be produced or maintained by the thing's own organization. That is part of what self-production means, so the agreement is built in, and A3 finds nothing.
- *Proposition.* Either A3 is false or it is empty.

**Shared floor.** Both sides accept F3: agreement built in by definition is no finding. Both accept the robot's facts: its parts are made and repaired by people.

**Split.** "Independent" → (i) defined without the other test; (ii) sharing no content with it.

- (i) still holds under the strict reading. Definition 11 does not mention the seven works.
- (ii) holds only in part. Condition (b) shares content with Definition 11. But Definition 11 asks, once and for all, that a thing make and replace *all* its components, its boundary included. Condition (b) is asked one work at a time, it can be met in part (grade 1), and it says nothing about the boundary unless the boundary work is checked on its own. So the two tests can still come apart, and A3 can still fail. X2's "empty" is too strong: A3 is less of a discovery, but it is not built in.

**Judgment.** On the loose reading, A3 breaks (X1). On the revised D-work, the robot grades 0 on every work and agrees with self-production, and A3 survives with a smaller but real claim.

**A consequence to flag.** By the same reasoning, today's AI systems, whose hardware is made and maintained by people, perform none of the seven works as one and are not wholes in the book's sense. This says nothing about whether they think (D-I, Scholium 2) or feel (A2).

**Verdict: splits.**

- *Survivor.* A3 as stated, read with D-work's revised (b), with the self-recharging robot added as a hard case (predicted degree 0) and a new paragraph, "How far the two tests are apart".
- *Broken reading.* A3 with (b) read as a loop of cause and effect alone.
- *Reason.* The robot breaks the loose reading. The strict reading costs some independence, not all of it.

## 9. PR1 (pattern by requirement)

**Old thesis (in short).** Seven requirements, one per work, each stated with "must", all of one kind: boundary, control, energy, transport, signaling, defense, and memory ("to rebuild its parts as they wear out, it must keep a record of how they are made").

**For (F).**

- F1. A requirement is conditional: if a thing is to last as one, it does this work (PR1's own wording uses "must").
- F2. Each work answers a distinct requirement of self-producing things, and that explains why the same works recur in different forms.
- *Proposition.* So the seven recur because the requirements recur.

**Against (X).**

- X1. Boundary is already in Definition 11 ("its boundary included"), so "it requires a boundary" is nearly true by definition.
- X2. In very small cells much transport is diffusion (A3, hard cases), so transport is required only where distances are large.
- X3. Defense is required only where damaging agents are present, and signaling only where parts lie at a distance.
- X4. "Record" covers two claims. A separate stored record, like a genome, is not required: mutually catalytic assemblies with no sequence-based polymers pass on their composition, a "compositional genome" (Segré, Ben-Eli & Lancet 2000, a model). Some state that carries forward how parts are made is had by anything that rebuilds its parts at all, so on that reading the requirement is nearly empty.
- *Proposition.* So PR1's seven requirements are not of one kind, and one of them is either false or nearly empty.

**Shared floor.** Both sides accept F1, and PR1's own lesson from its earlier round: a requirement that holds by definition is empty.

**Split.** "Required" → (a) nearly true by definition; (b) general: holds of any self-producing thing; (c) holding only in some circumstances.

- Boundary: the edge itself is (a). That the edge sorts, letting some things across and turning others back, is (b).
- Energy and control: (b).
- Transport, signaling, defense: (c), each with its circumstance named.

**Split.** "Record" → (i) some state that carries forward how parts are made: (b), and nearly empty; (ii) a separate stored record: not required (X4). It is a finding about every known living thing.

**Verdict: splits.**

- *Survivors.* The thesis, restated in three groups: general (energy, control, a sorting edge); holding in some circumstances (transport, signaling, defense); and memory as some state that carries forward how parts are made. "What would count against it" now says which cases count against which requirement.
- *Broken reading.* "Must keep a record", read as a separate stored record.
- *Reason.* The requirements differ in strength, and saying so keeps each from claiming more than it can bear.

## 10. A4 (the society I live in is a whole to a degree)

**Old declared reading.** "'A society' here means a present-day state society: a population under one government, with territory, law and institutions."

**Old boundary test.** "dissolve the state and no one sorts what crosses on the society's behalf".

**For (F).**

- F1. Each row has evidence that the society performs the work as one (borders, government and markets, food and power, roads, media, public health, archives and schools).
- F2. The take-apart test separates what the society does from what its members do separately.
- F3. I depend on several rows and take part in some.
- *Proposition.* So the society is a whole to a degree, and I am a member.

**Against (X).**

- X1. Society and state come apart. On Leeson's account, when Somalia's central government collapsed in 1991, trade, money, telecommunications and much dispute settlement went on through clans, customary law, firms and markets, and Somalia did better on most of eighteen development indicators in the years after the collapse than before it, though it stayed poor (Leeson 2007). So a take-apart test that "dissolves the state" mistakes the state for the society, and several rows are carried by institutions that are not the state.
- X2. Prices coordinate without anyone directing the whole (Hayek 1945), so control is not performed as one.
- X3. Members' interests conflict, so no row is performed as one (Queller & Strassmann 2009).
- *Proposition.* So the reading misidentifies the whole, and some rows fail.

**Shared floor.** Both sides accept F1's facts, and that the take-apart test is the standard for "as one" (D-work, Definition 5).

**Split.** "Society" → (i) the state; (ii) the population under one government with all its institutions, state and non-state.

- On (i), X1 succeeds. Several works went on without a state.
- On (ii), the works X1 describes went on in the society's non-state institutions, which (ii) counts, so X1 supports A4 on that reading.

**The rest.**

- X2: control in D-work needs no single controller, only combining done by structures the society's organization keeps up (money, property law, exchanges). Hayek's point is the reason markets are in the control row. It does not count against the row.
- X3: conflict holds a grade at 1 (D-whole, Definition 7), which A4 already expects.

**Verdict: holds with revision.**

- *Surviving wording.* Declared reading: "a population under one government, with its territory and all its institutions, state and non-state ... The state is one of its institutions, not the society itself." Boundary test: "disperse the institutions that sort what crosses (border posts, customs, immigration offices)". A new scholium, "State and society", cites Leeson.
- *Reason.* The whole is the society, and the state is one of its institutions.

## 11. P1 (a whole at one level, a part at the next)

**Old step 6 (in part).** "By D-I, 'I' names the doer of each thought, and A2 identifies that doer with this body."

**For (F).** The demonstration as written: I am a human body of cells (A2); a multicellular organism performs all seven works as one (A3); so it is a whole (D-whole, Definition 8), with cells as members (Definition 9); the society performs all seven as one, and I am a member (A4); the I is the same in both (step 6); so I am a whole at one level and a part at the next.

**Against (X).**

- X1. Step 6 rests on A2's identity of the I with this body. A2 no longer scores identity (section 4), so step 6 has a gap.
- X2. "Part" covers a component (the arm is part of the body) and a member (D-whole, Definition 9).
- *Proposition.* So P1's conclusion rests on an unscored premise, or on a pun.

**Shared floor.** Both sides accept A2's scored content: this thinking goes with this body, and that body is the same whole across the times at issue (Definition 12).

**Split.** "I am" → (a1) made of, and goes with; (a2) identical with.

- On (a1), the demonstration is valid. Step 6 needs only that the thinking of each thought goes with this one body, which is the same whole throughout. Then the whole of step 3 and the member of step 5 are the same body, and that is the body this thinking goes with.
- On (a2), it is valid too, and says the same of me without qualification. That reading is declared.
- X2: P1's reading already fixes "part" as member. No pun is used.

**Verdict: splits** (following A2).

- *Survivor 1 (scored).* The body I am made of, the one this thinking goes with, is a whole whose members are cells, and a member of the society I live in.
- *Survivor 2 (declared).* I am a whole at one level and a part at the next, with "am" as identity.
- *Wording.* The statement is unchanged. Step 6 now reads "by A2, clause (a), this thinking goes with this body", and a new Scholium 4 says which reading is scored.
- *Reason.* The demonstration needs only what A2 scores, and the identity reading rides along as declared.

## 12. P2 (a collective mind happens)

**Old statement.** "A collective mind happens in the society I live in."

**For (F).**

- F1. The society performs control, signaling and memory as one, at least in part (A4).
- F2. "Collective mind" is defined as control performed as one by a society, together with the signaling and memory that control uses.
- *Proposition.* So a collective mind, so defined, happens.

**Against (X).**

- X1. "Mind" covers three claims: (i) control in the book's sense; (ii) something felt, a first-person side; (iii) Spinoza's mind, the idea of a body (E2P13).
- X2. A familiar word given a new descriptive meaning keeps the force of its old one: a persuasive definition (Stevenson 1938). A reader hears (ii) or (iii) in "a collective mind happens".
- *Proposition.* So P2's statement claims more than its demonstration shows.

**Shared floor.** Both sides accept F1 and F2 as stated, and that the demonstration shows (i) only (P2, step 6).

**Split.** "Mind" → (i), (ii), (iii).

- (i) holds, from A4 and the definition.
- (ii) stays open. Nothing in the core concerns a first-person description of a society (P2, Scholium 2).
- (iii) holds only if a society is an individual in Spinoza's sense, which the core does not decide.
- X2 is right about the old statement, which said "a collective mind" without saying which sense.

**Verdict: splits.**

- *Survivor.* "A collective mind, in the sense defined here, happens in the society I live in."
- *Open.* The felt sense (ii); Spinoza's sense (iii), which depends on a society being an individual.
- *Wording.* The statement now carries "in the sense defined here". A new Scholium 4 sets out the three senses and Stevenson's point.
- *Reason.* The name invited two claims the demonstration does not make. Whether to rename it is README open question 7, now sharper.
