**Definition.** A whole is a bounded system whose boundary works as a filter and whose parts share a fate. Something is a whole to the degree that its boundary admits what the whole needs and excludes what harms it, and to the degree that its parts cooperate and rarely conflict. Wholeness is a matter of degree, not a yes-or-no property.

**Membership.** A part belongs to a whole to the degree that its fate is tied to the whole's fate, whatever its origin. Mitochondria descend from once free-living bacteria and are now full members of the cell (Sagan 1967). Gut bacteria are about as numerous as the body's own cells (Sender, Fuchs & Milo 2016), are not the body's own cells, and still do work the body relies on, including shaping the immune system (Belkaid & Hand 2014). Membership is judged by function, not origin.

**Vantage.** A whole looks porous from inside and unified from above. From within a level one sees the traffic, the conflict, and the outsiders; from the level above, the same activity reads as one steady function. Both descriptions are true of the same system at different scales.

**Use.** A2 describes the person as a whole whose parts are cells. A3 and A4 describe the person as a part of a larger whole. By this definition, both hold at once: the I is a whole at one level and a part at the next. The degree of wholeness is measured by the cooperation-and-conflict criterion of Queller & Strassmann (2009), who treat organismality as high cooperation and very low conflict among parts.

**Limit.** This is a definition. It says what the terms mean; it does not raise the confidence of A2, A3 or A4. In particular, the vantage clause explains why a society's boundary and defense rows may look weak to us as its members, but those rows still earn their scores from their own evidence.
