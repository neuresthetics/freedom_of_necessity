**Claim.** Society exhibits organism-level properties across seven rows: boundary, control, energy, transport, signaling, defense, memory. Each row names a function any persisting whole must perform; the expression differs completely at each level (see PR1). Each row earns its confidence separately, and A3 is capped by its weakest row.

**Backbone.** Miller, *Living Systems* (1978), argues that living systems at every level, from cell to society, carry the same set of critical subsystems (boundary, decider, ingestor, converter, distributor, channel and net, memory, among others). The rows below follow Miller except where noted.

**Evidence by row**

- **Boundary** (membrane : skin : border). The cell membrane is a selectively permeable lipid bilayer that controls what enters and leaves (Singer & Nicolson 1972). Skin does the same for the body, and Miller lists a boundary subsystem at the society level: borders, customs, and immigration control, which decide who and what gets in.
- **Control** (gene regulation : nervous system : governance). Cells regulate their behavior through gene-regulatory networks; the body's decider is the nervous system; Miller's society-level decider is government. Seeley's work on honeybee swarm decisions (*Honeybee Democracy*, 2010) shows a collective reaching one decision with no central controller, which is the pattern this row needs at the group level.
- **Energy** (mitochondria : digestion and metabolism : energy economy). Cells convert nutrients to ATP in mitochondria; bodies digest and metabolize; societies capture and convert energy through agriculture, fuels, and power grids. White (1943) and Smil (*Energy and Civilization*, 2017) treat energy capture as the base on which social organization is built.
- **Transport** (cytoskeleton and vesicles : circulation : roads and logistics). Materials move inside cells along the cytoskeleton, through bodies via the circulatory system, and through societies over transport networks. West, Brown & Enquist (1997) derive biological scaling laws from the geometry of distribution networks, and Bettencourt et al. (2007) find that urban infrastructure networks (road surface, cable length) also scale sublinearly with city size, which is quantitative evidence that cities scale as organisms do. For the socioeconomic side, see caveat 4.
- **Signaling** (ligand and receptor : nerves and hormones : language and media). Cells communicate by chemical signals binding receptors (Alberts et al., *Molecular Biology of the Cell*, 6th ed., 2015); bodies use the nervous and endocrine systems; societies use language, writing, and media, which is Miller's channel-and-net subsystem. Honeybee dance communication (von Frisch 1967) is a clear group-level case of coded signals coordinating a collective.
- **Defense** (cell-autonomous immunity : immune system : public health and social immunity). Miller has no separate defense subsystem; he folds protection into the boundary, so this row rests on other sources. Individual cells defend themselves against pathogens (Randow, MacMicking & James 2013, "cell-autonomous immunity"). The body has its immune system. Social insect colonies show colony-level disease defense through hygiene, grooming, and isolating infected members (Cremer, Armitage & Schmid-Hempel 2007; Cremer, Pull & Fürst 2018, "social immunity"). In humans the job is done at two levels. Individuals avoid likely sources of infection through disgust (Schaller & Park 2011, "the behavioral immune system"); that source supports this avoidance half only. Societies add organized public health: quarantine and isolation have been used against epidemics from the plague of the 14th century to influenza (Tognotti 2013), and Rosen (1958) traces the history of public health, including sanitation and vaccination, as a collective defense against disease.
- **Memory** (DNA and epigenetic marks : neural memory : records and institutions). Cells store information in DNA and hold their identity through epigenetic marks; brains store memory through lasting synaptic change (Kandel 2001); societies store it in writing, archives, law, and institutions. Halbwachs (1925) argues that memory is carried by social frameworks rather than individuals alone.

**Caveats**

1. Person to society is not a single step. Persons act through groups, organizations, and communities, as cells act through tissues and organs (Miller's intermediate levels). Every row passes through those levels, and A4 carries the same discount.
2. The strongest superorganism cases (Wheeler 1911; Hölldobler & Wilson, *The Superorganism*, 2008) are social insect colonies. Queller & Strassmann (2009) define organismality as high cooperation and very low conflict among the parts. Close kinship is one route to that condition, as in near-clonal body cells and many insect colonies, but it is not required: they list clonality and high relatedness among traits that are not essential to organisms, and they count some mutualistic associations as organisms. Persons in a society cooperate extensively but keep their own interests and conflict, so human societies score lower on the low-conflict axis. The analogy is therefore weaker for human societies.
3. Society's boundary is porous, its control is distributed, and its signaling is not unified. These are real differences in expression, and each row is scored on whether the function is necessary, not on surface resemblance.
4. Bettencourt et al. (2007) themselves discuss how cities differ from organisms. Urban socioeconomic quantities (wages, GDP, patents, and other measures of wealth creation and innovation) scale superlinearly with population (exponent about 1.2), so the pace of urban life speeds up as cities grow. Biological metabolic scaling is sublinear (exponent below 1), so the pace of life slows as organisms grow. Only urban infrastructure scales sublinearly like biology. The scaling evidence therefore supports the shared distribution constraint in the Transport row, not a general identity between cities and organisms.
