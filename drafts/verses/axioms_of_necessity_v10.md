# Axioms of Necessity (v10)

*A verse companion to* Freedom of Necessity

> The verses are numbered, and the notes in Part Two use the same numbers. *Italic lines* state the book's thesis on freedom. † marks a norm the book doesn't derive; ‡ marks a claim still waiting for its evidence. How the book underneath is built is set out in HOW_THIS_BOOK_IS_BUILT.md.


# Part One: The Verses

## I. God, or Nature

*One order, and no outside.*

1. There is one order, and it has no outside. Whatever is, is in it. Call it God, or Nature.

2. The order is not a whole among wholes. It has no boundary, because there is nothing outside it to sort.

3. Every event is caused, a choice included.

4. Nothing in the order aims. What looks like purpose is function: the work by which a whole keeps itself going.

5. Selection shaped each function, and nobody aimed it.

6. God, in this sense, has no plans for us. Asked by cable whether he believed in God, Einstein answered: in Spinoza's God, who shows himself in the orderly harmony of what exists, not in a God concerned with the fates and doings of human beings.

7. The one order shows itself to us in two ways, as bodies and as the ideas of bodies. So one event can have two true descriptions, no view stands outside the order, and only what is caused by nothing else is free without degree.

8. A human being can't fail to be a part of nature.


## II. The Whole

*Inside the one order, some things hold together.*

9. A whole is a boundary that sorts, and members that share a fate.

10. A membrane doesn't decide what enters. It sorts, by what it is made of.

11. Wholeness comes by degrees, two ways at once: how finely the edge sorts, and how little the members fight. No line divides a whole from a heap.

12. A stone lets almost nothing through, and what little passes, it doesn't sort. Its parts want nothing, so the question of their quarrel doesn't arise. A stone is whole only a little, and only at its edge.

13. How whole a thing is, and how sure we are of it, are two questions. Never answer one with the other.

14. A hand cut from the body is a hand in name only.

15. Once joined, parts and whole shape each other. The parts may come first; they don't stay what they were.

16. Many of life's great steps came when things that once copied themselves alone began to copy themselves only together: genes in cells, cells in bodies, insects in colonies.

17. Strangers can become members. The mitochondrion was once a free-living bacterium. To be a member is to share a fate, whatever your origin.

18. A guest works beside a whole without sharing its fate. A member shares it. Some members were guests first.

19. Lasting wholes tend to be built from stable smaller wholes. Every whole lives inside something: as a member, as a guest, or only in the order.

20. Within a group, the selfish win.  
    Between groups, the generous win.  
    Every whole with living members lives inside that tension.

21. A whole is held together by what flows between its members as much as by what they are made of.

22. A worm in the blood would take each drop for a world and never see the body. We live in our corner of nature as that worm lives in the blood. What it knows is true of what it touches; it turns false only when taken for a picture of the whole.

23. Seen from among its members, a whole looks like a crowd; seen from a distance, like one steady organization. Both views are partial, both are true, and neither outranks the other.


## III. Time and Change

*The river keeps its name.*

24. A body keeps its nature while its parts are replaced, so long as the new parts keep the same proportion of motion and rest among themselves.

25. Most of your cells are younger than a year; a few of your neurons are as old as you. The shape stays; the matter flows.

26. A mind grows by taking in what fits and by reshaping itself around what doesn't.

27. Every birth is a new beginning inside the old order.


## IV. The Seven Works

*What a living whole is found doing.*

28. A cell and a person each do seven works: they keep a boundary, steer, feed, carry, signal, defend, and remember. Whether a people does all seven, as one, is the question.

29. Steering is mostly spread out: through networks of genes in a cell, scouts in a swarm, self-governing groups in a people. Where it gathers, as in a brain, it still runs on signals from the members.

30. A swarm of bees finds a new home with no one in charge. Scouts dance their reports, rival reports compete, and a quorum ends the contest.

31. A regulator needs at least as much variety as what it regulates. Only variety can absorb variety.

32. Every living thing is a small room of order, paid for with free energy taken in and disorder carried out.

33. The blood goes round; the food goes through. So does most of what the world makes: less than a tenth of what enters comes back around.

34. A signal is a difference that makes a difference: it lessens the receiver's doubt.

35. Some guests are enemies. Bodies defend themselves, and peoples did too, long before medicine: first disgust and avoidance, then quarantine.

36. To remember is to be changed. To copy is to promise a future.

37. To copy itself, a thing must carry a description of itself, and a machine that can read and copy it.

38. A work belongs to a people only when the people does it, not each member alone. A crowd that shrinks from the sick is not yet a quarantine.

39. A stone lasts, a hurricane feeds, an archive remembers. Each should fall short of some work. If one passed all seven, the seven would be too loose to find anything.


## V. Pattern by Requirement

*Same requirement, different clothes.*

40. Where the same requirement holds at different scales, a matching work tends to appear, dressed differently at each.

41. Likeness proves nothing, and a requirement only says where to look. What carries across is the work, found done at each level.

42. A city is not a body. As a body grows, its pace of life slows; as a city grows, its wages and inventions speed up.

43. The petal was once a leaf. That is kinship, not function.


## VI. Life and Ecology

*Each life in its own world, all in one web.*

44. The tick's world holds little more than warmth, the smell of butyric acid, and touch. A boundary filters the world into a world.

45. Poison sprayed on one part of the living web turns up in others, concentrating as it climbs the food chain.

46. The difference between our minds and other animals' is one of degree, not kind.

47. ‡ If the biosphere is a whole, it is one of low degree: it has no sharp edge, only a thinning into space, and its members never stop contending. How far life steers the conditions it lives in is still argued.

48. The human economy lives inside the biosphere, not the other way round. It runs on ordered matter and energy it can't make.

49. † A thing is right when it tends to keep the living community intact, stable, and beautiful.


## VII. Health and Disease

*The works holding, and the works failing, inside one body.*

50. The body keeps its inner sea steady while the world outside changes, and much of that steadiness is foresight: it adjusts before the demand arrives.

51. Health is not only fitting your surroundings. It is room to adapt when they change.

52. Cancer is cells breaking the many-celled bargain: dividing out of turn, taking more than their share, deaf to the signal to stop.

53. Defense is a filter tuned by history, not a verdict on worth. Whether it sorts self from other, or answers to danger, is still argued.

54. A pump on Broad Street carried cholera. A people's roads are also its diseases' roads.

55. ‡ Medicine is a social science, and politics is nothing else but medicine on a large scale.


## VIII. The Self

*Here the book begins: with me.*

56. I, a human, exist. The claim is existence, not permanence: I exist as a process.

57. The floating man, touching nothing, still knows that he exists. From this, Ibn Sina concluded that the self is a substance apart from the body; I don't follow him there.

58. I am many things bound into one, a whole to my cells and a part of my people. I stand at the seam.

59. I contain multitudes: some thirty trillion human cells, and roughly as many bacteria.

60. The mind is the idea of the body; whatever happens in the body, the mind perceives in its own way.

61. No one has yet determined what the body can do.

62. "I" is where the many are counted as one. Most of what I do is never counted.

63. We know more than we can tell. The body rides the bicycle; the person can't say how. Habit is memory that has moved into the body, where it works without asking.

64. Nearly every cell with a nucleus carries the whole genome and reads only its own page.

65. A young neuron lives only if it is needed: it dies unless the tissue it reaches answers.

66. Looking inward, Hume found only a bundle of perceptions; the Buddha taught that no lasting self is found among a person's parts. I claim nothing lasting. I part from them only if "no self" means "no I at all."

67. If the self is a continuity across changing parts, what matters in survival is that continuity more than strict identity.


## IX. Knowledge

*Every knower stands somewhere.*

68. There are three kinds of knowing: opinion, from hearsay and loose experience; reason, working from notions common to all things; and intuition, which sees a thing from its essence.

69. It is the nature of reason to regard things as necessary, under a certain form of eternity.

70. Because the order has no outside, every knower stands inside it. A vantage is a location, not a flaw.

71. *No outside description delivers what it is like to be a bat. No inside feeling delivers the causal chain. Neither outranks the other.*

72. Spinoza held that whoever has a true idea knows that he has it. Here these verses part from him: apart from the first, every claim can be lowered.


## X. Affect

*Knowing changes feeling.*

73. An affect is a change in the body's power of acting, together with the idea of that change.

74. Joy is the mind's passage to a greater perfection; sadness, its passage to a lesser.

75. Each thing, as far as it is in itself, strives to persist in its being, and that striving is its very essence, not a goal it holds in view.

76. We don't desire a thing because we judge it good. We call it good because we desire it. Good and bad name nothing positive in things; they are ways of comparing.

77. A feeling is the felt side of an affect: the mind's reading of the body's state.

78. The brain regions that register your heartbeat overlap with the salience network, which is proposed to switch the brain between inward and outward work.

79. Reason doesn't overrule a passion. It works by becoming a stronger affect.

80. A passion stops being a passion once we form a clear and distinct idea of it, and the mind has more power over the affects the more it understands things as necessary.

81. Understanding a cause moves us less by it and more from it. From the third kind of knowledge comes the intellectual love of God, and blessedness is that love.


## XI. Freedom

*One act, two true descriptions.*

82. *Every choice is one event with two descriptions. Seen from outside, it is a cause in the chain. Felt from inside, it is mine. Neither outranks the other.*

83. *The inside description isn't idle. For many questions it is the better cause: it names what made the difference.*

84. ‡ A candidate correlate: in the brain, weaving many signals into one act may run through three networks, one turned inward, one turned to the task, and one switching between them. Why any of it is felt at all remains undecided.

85. Freedom that is only an inner cause at work, Kant said, is the freedom of a turnspit, which, once wound up, turns by itself. *But a turnspit doesn't understand what moves it.*

86. Freedom is not an escape from necessity. Strictly, only what exists and acts from its own nature alone is free, and only God is like that. Finite things are free by degrees.

87. *We act when we are the adequate cause of what happens, and we are passive when we are only a partial cause. The more of what you do follows from your own nature, the freer you are.*

88. People think they are free because they know their actions and not the causes. A stone set moving, if it could think, would believe it moved by its own wish.

89. *What dies is the uncaused will. What lives is the inside view: still mine, only not uncaused.*

90. *Freedom doesn't need that you could have done otherwise, given all that came before. It needs that what you did came from you.*

91. *To understand a cause is to know where it came from: genes, schooling, or a meddler's hand. A cause seen at its source is no longer the same cause, because seeing changes it. Sometimes it loosens; sometimes it steadies into resolve. And sometimes you see it whole and are still bound, because it is necessary. Freedom is not escaping the cause. It is knowing it, origin and all, and acting from that knowledge.*

92. *No finite thing is its own cause, so no one is ultimately responsible. Responsibility then looks forward: to repair, to restraint, to learning.*

93. The signal that seemed to come before a choice may be noise drifting to a threshold, not a verdict reached in the dark. *And even if it were a verdict, an unconscious weaving is still mine.*

94. A person acts freely, it is said, when moved by a desire they want to be moved by. What speaks for the person is not a higher desire but a clearer understanding.

95. *Freedom is the insight into necessity. To understand a passion clearly is to make it an action, and so to become more fully its cause.*

96. Cook Ding's knife stayed sharp for nineteen years because it followed the grain of the ox. Skill is freedom found inside necessity.

97. Become who you are.


## XII. Language and Signal

*Signals between members, from bees to words.*

98. A sign means through its differences from other signs.

99. For a large class of cases, the meaning of a word is its use in the language.

100. Some words don't report an act; they are the act. "I promise" makes a promise.

101. The bee dances the way to the flowers.


## XIII. The People

*At the seam, I reach my people through my groups.*

102. I act on my people through groups, as a cell acts on the body through organs.

103. The picture is old: the belly and the limbs; the eye that can't say to the hand, "I have no need of you"; the commonwealth drawn as a giant made of citizens; the body seen as a state of cells.

104. If a people is a whole, it is one of low degree: its boundary leaks and its members quarrel, and yet it goes on. Your own boundary leaks too.

105. Some groups hold together by likeness; others by difference, as organs do.

106. ‡ A people holds together while its members share a fate. Where some do the work and others keep the energy, cohesion falls.

107. A commons can last with no ruler and no owner, when its users draw clear bounds, watch one another, settle quarrels cheaply, and are left free to organize, often inside larger rings of rule.

108. Nothing is more useful to a human than another human.

109. A people writes itself into bodies through habit and ritual.

110. The social contract was always a thought experiment; who ever actually consented? The cells never signed anything either.

111. ‡ When the norms that hold a person to the whole go slack, despair rises.

112. When an organ forgets the person, the person becomes a guest in their own people.

113. Evil can be done by a member who has stopped thinking.

114. Napoleon, Tolstoy thought, didn't steer history; he was carried by the movements of millions.

115. A people's mind is not a thing it owns. It happens, the way a whirlpool happens in a river: feedback among many, working as one. A happening exists; that it feels is not claimed.

116. ‡ The circle of concern can widen, from kin to strangers to other animals.

117. A people can remember what no one alive lived through: a fourth-century text held the clue to artemisinin. It can also carry wounds that no living person caused.


## XIV. Economy

*Feeding, carrying, and signaling, at the scale of a people.*

118. Ten who split the work make far more pins than ten who don't.

119. Prices carry knowledge no one holds whole. Word that something has grown scarce reaches people who never learn why.

120. A gift creates the obligations to receive and to return, and the circuit binds the givers together.

121. What is prudent for one household can harm all when every household does it at once.

122. Private vices make public benefits, said one. What isn't good for the swarm isn't good for the bee, said another. They were answering at different levels.

123. Large groups struggle to supply shared goods, because each member gains from the good whether or not they pay for it.


## XV. Conflict and the Freedom of a People

*From the person to the people, each step marked.*

124. ‡ A whole that won't defend itself rarely lasts.

125. ‡ The striving to persist, which grounds a person's virtue, may carry over to a people, when a people acts as if guided by one mind.

126. Cooperation among self-interested members can arise when they meet again and again and answer in kind.

127. ‡ Reconciliation can turn quarreling members back toward a shared fate.

128. † Restrain a member only to prevent harm to others.

129. The Grand Inquisitor offers bread in exchange for freedom, and claims people will gladly make the trade.

130. ‡ A people is freer as more of its members act from their own nature, and how many can is partly luck.

131. No substantial famine, Sen observed, has struck an independent democracy with a relatively free press.

132. † The true end of the state, Spinoza wrote, is freedom.

133. Many free voices, one whole: counterpoint, a people done well.


## XVI. Mind and Machine

*Do the same measures hold for minds we build?*

134. Spots and stripes need no painter: two chemicals, reacting and spreading, are enough.

135. Can a machine think? One proposed a game of imitation; another answered that running a program isn't understanding. Undecided.

136. ‡ Test a machine as you'd test anything: how finely its edge sorts, and, if its parts have interests of their own, how little they fight.

137. ‡ If an AI ever has an A1 of its own, "I, an AI, exist," its A2 won't be about cells.

138. To dismiss a machine's opinion as merely subjective is to grant it a place to stand. That shows a point of view at work, not one that is felt; whether anything is felt there stays undecided.

139. † Build machines whose parts share our fate.


## XVII. Art

*Artists who saw works recur, and where likeness misled them.*

140. Leonardo saw the earth as a body whose waters are its blood. The carrying was real; the veins were likeness.

141. A woman who would write needs money and a room of her own with a lock on the door. A part needs a boundary of its own.

142. To be in uncertainties and doubts without irritable reaching after fact and reason: the poet's gift is the method's undecided.


## XVIII. Death

*Every whole ends. The order doesn't.*

143. To philosophize is to learn to die, said one. A free person thinks of nothing less than death, answered another; his wisdom is a meditation on life.

144. Where death is, I am not; where I am, death is not.

145. The body is mortal; the germ line goes on.

146. A cell can take itself apart cleanly on a signal, and the body is partly shaped that way: the webbing between embryonic fingers dies back.

147. Something of the mind is eternal, Spinoza said. Undecided.

148. Death is not a failed boundary. It is a whole's term ending, inside an order that doesn't end. The river ends; the water goes on.


## XIX. Method

*How sure is any of this?*

149. Only the start is beyond revision: I, a human, exist. Everything else stands on something, and stands no higher than what holds it up.

150. Human actions can be treated as if they were lines, planes, and bodies.

151. The definitions stand first on the page but were found last, worked back from what the claims needed, and that is said openly. Only the start is first both ways.

152. An explanation is a lamp, not a proof. It shows where to look, and it tests what is offered as evidence; only evidence that holds up under the light settles what you find.

153. A claim that nothing could lower isn't a claim. The start is the one exception.

154. If you want your confidence to match the evidence, then when the evidence runs out, say undecided. Undecided is a state, not a failure.

155. The easiest whole to fool is yourself.


## XX. Ethics at the Seam †

*Good and bad are ways of comparing, and the core has no ethics axiom.*

156. From striving comes a good: what is useful to our persisting. That is prudence, not an ought, and these verses stop there.

157. † A person is where the cells' good and the people's good meet, and ethics begins at that seam.

158. † What the good seek for themselves, they want for others too.

159. † There is peace where a whole's members aren't at war. Suffering grows from clinging to what must change.


## XXI. Close

160. The cell, the person, the people: the same requirements, three tongues.

161. *Freedom is necessity clearly understood from inside. Seen from outside, it is necessity still, and neither outranks the other.*

162. All things excellent are as difficult as they are rare.

**Tiers**

The kinds of entry in the book itself (root, definition, axiom, principle, proposition, demonstration, corollary, scholium) are set out in [HOW_THIS_BOOK_IS_BUILT.md](../../HOW_THIS_BOOK_IS_BUILT.md), and that front piece is the authority. A verse that restates an entry takes that entry's kind. The remaining labels are for what a verse can be that an entry isn't. The extra labels core, method, formal and report still wait on your confirmation (open items).

*Kinds from the front piece*
- **Root:** A1, "I, a human, exist." The single starting point, taken as given, and the only claim that can't be revised (56, 149).
- **Definition:** fixes what a word means. It is judged on clarity and fit, supports nothing on its own, and makes no claim more likely. Verses 1, 2, 7 and 8 are definitions, and so are the verses that restate D-whole. Definitions were found downstream, from what the claims needed a word to mean, and then placed first; this is said openly (151; front piece §5).
- **Axiom:** a starting claim about the world that carries its own evidence and can be lowered. *Empirical* (A2) or *model* (A3, A4: one level said to correspond to another, judged work by work, and no stronger than the weakest). Verses 3 and 4 are candidate axioms (entries 13 and 14), not yet entries.
- **Principle:** explains why claims line up, and adds no confidence (PR1; 40–41, 152).
- **Proposition:** derived from the entries it names, and no stronger than the weakest of them (P1; 115).
- Demonstrations and corollaries have no verses of their own. These notes work like scholia: they explain and compare, and prove and score nothing.

*Labels for verses*
- **Core:** a verse that restates a committed entry (A1–A4, D-whole, PR1, P1). The note names the entry and its kind from the list above. It is scored there, not here.
- **Method:** a rule of METHOD. Its imperatives are conditional ("if you want confidence to match evidence, then…"), not ethical norms.
- **Empirical:** a finding with published support. **Formal:** a mathematical or logical result.
- **Model:** a claim resting on a published scientific model with support. This is not the front piece's "axiom (model)," which is a correspondence between levels. **Model conjecture:** something a model suggests but no entry supports yet, including extending a thinker's claim to a level they didn't address. Marked ‡ on the page.
- **Thesis:** the free-will thesis or a direct consequence of it, set in italics on the page.
- **Needs evidence:** a causal claim that needs its own entry before it can be scored. Marked ‡.
- **Not derived:** a norm, or a report of one, that the core doesn't ground, because there is no ethics axiom. Marked †. The whole of Chapter XX is not derived.
- **Verse:** poetry, not a claim.
- **Report:** a thinker's view, stated because it matters. The note says whether the book agrees ("compare"), disagrees ("contrast"), or leaves it open.
- **Reading ours / author's position:** the book's own interpretation or argument, not a finding. Where the position is Jason's, the note says so (91, 115, 138, 151).

**Precedence.** A verse's own label wins over its chapter's label. Where a verse mixes kinds, the note labels each part (for example "report + verse"), and an inline italic sentence is thesis.

**Citation words.**
- *Reading ours:* the interpretation is the book's.
- *Grouping ours:* the arrangement is the book's.
- *After X:* drawn loosely from X.
- *Compare X:* a parallel, not an endorsement.
- *Contrast X / against X:* set against X's own view.

**House rules**
1. **The start.** A1, "I, a human, exist," is the only claim that can't be revised. Every other line can be lowered, and definitions support nothing alone. The definitions were found downstream and set first; only A1 comes first in both orders (151).
2. **No aiming.** Boundaries filter; they don't choose. "Choose" and "choice" appear only in the free-will thesis and in axiom 3, which the thesis rests on.
3. **Vantage vs. inside.** A vantage, among the members or at a distance, is not the thesis's inside/outside. **Inside** always means the felt, first-person side. The book assumes the thesis (see 82): the two descriptions of a choice are both true, and neither outranks the other.
4. **The order is not a whole among wholes.**
5. **Wholeness** has two dimensions and no threshold: how selective the boundary is (discrimination, not impermeability), and how little the members with interests conflict. **Where the parts have no interests, the conflict dimension is undefined (not applicable), not zero** (the stone, 12). Not yet confirmed by you (open items).
6. **Confidence.** The weakest-support cap measures confidence, never wholeness.
7. **Two kinds of necessity.** **Necessity** is modal (couldn't be otherwise), and **requirement** is hypothetical (what a whole must do to last). The title uses the first; PR1 uses the second.
8. **Fair attribution.** No invented quotes, and paraphrases are marked.
9. **Performed, not required.** A3 claims only that the seven works are *performed*: by the cell, by the person, and by a people as a collective, not merely by its members separately. That they *must* be performed is PR1's thesis, which explains and adds no confidence. Verses and notes say "does" or "performed" for A3 and keep "must" for PR1.


### I. God, or Nature
- **1.** *definition.* Supports nothing alone. E1P15; *Deus sive Natura*, E4 Preface.
- **2.** *definition.* Supports nothing alone. Partial agreement with Spinoza: substance is indivisible and not made of parts (E1P12–P13, E1P15 Scholium). Contrast his whole of nature as one individual (E2 Lemma 7 Scholium), which is the order under extension, not the order itself. Reading ours.
- **3.** *axiom (candidate, entry 13; lowerable).* Weaker than Spinoza's own claim that nothing is contingent (E1P29, E1P33), which the book reports but doesn't need. The thesis needs only that choices are caused. If quantum indeterminacy reaches the brain, it would add chance, not freedom. Contested; lowered by any evidence of uncaused events at the scale of choices.
- **4.** *axiom (candidate, entry 14; lowerable).* E1 Appendix (final causes as human figments); against Aristotle's final cause. Function in the organizational sense: a function is a contribution to the self-maintenance of the organization that produces it (Mossio, Saborido & Moreno 2009). This also supplies non-moral norms of working and failing (see 51–52).
- **5.** *empirical.* Darwin 1859; Pittendrigh 1958 coined *teleonomy* for apparent purpose without aim; Monod, *Chance and Necessity* 1970. Selection explains a function's history; 4 says what a function is.
- **6.** *report.* Einstein to Rabbi Herbert Goldstein, April 1929; *New York Times*, 25 April 1929, p. 60. Paraphrased. The name stays because the cable is the image.
- **7.** *definition (reading ours).* The pantheism doing work. (a) Two descriptions: thought and extension are attributes of one substance, so the order of ideas is the order of things (E2P1–P2, E2P7 and Scholium). (b) No view from outside: E1P15 with 1. (c) Top of the freedom scale: only God is free in the strict sense (E1D7, E1P17 Corollary 2). Finite things rise toward it by degrees, and the wise mind's freedom is sketched at E5P42 Scholium. Supports nothing alone; the thesis's evidence is entries 5a, 5b and 6a.
- **8.** *definition.* E4P4 (Elwes: "It is impossible, that man should not be a part of Nature"). "Part" here means a thing in the order, not a member of a whole (see the glossary). Supports nothing alone.

### II. The Whole
- **9.** *definition (core: D-whole).* "Sorts" means the boundary is selective (see the glossary).
- **10.** *empirical.* In a cell membrane, most of the sorting is done by channel and transporter proteins (Singer & Nicolson 1972; Alberts et al., *Molecular Biology of the Cell*). Boundaries filter; they don't aim.
- **11.** *definition + model.* D-whole for the boundary dimension. Queller & Strassmann 2009 for cooperation and conflict. "Members that fight" means members with interests of their own (replicators, agents); where there are none, the conflict dimension is undefined (see 12). Graded all the way down, and useful for comparison, not classification. Rival measures exist: Krakauer et al. 2020 (information that persists over time) and integrated-information measures (Tononi). ## Glossary

Each term is used in one sense throughout.

- **Order / God, or Nature:** everything that is, with no outside and no boundary. It is not a whole. It has no will and no plans. It shows itself to us as bodies and as ideas of bodies (7). In "a part of nature" (8), "part" means a thing in the order, not a member of a whole.
- **Necessity (modal):** what couldn't be otherwise. It is used in the title, in "necessity understood," and at 69, 86 and 161.
- **Requirement (hypothetical):** what a whole must do if it is to last. PR1, "Pattern by requirement," holds that the seven works are requirements; that thesis explains and adds no confidence.
- **Performed:** done, as a matter of evidence. A3 claims only that the seven works are performed at each level; for a people, performed by the people as a collective, not merely by its members separately (38).
- **Caused:** brought about by something in the order. Axiom 3 says every event, a choice included, is caused.
- **Whole:** a boundary that sorts, plus members that share a fate (D-whole). Every whole sits inside something larger, at least the order.
- **Boundary / filter:** the edge of a whole. It lets some things through and turns others back because of what it is made of. It never aims.
- **Selective:** a boundary is selective to the degree that it *discriminates*, letting through what the whole needs and keeping out what harms it. Selective doesn't mean impermeable: a stone lets almost nothing through and so is barely selective.
- **Share a fate:** members share a fate to the degree that their prospects of persisting (or reproducing) rise and fall together with the whole's. Compare the fitness alignment of Queller & Strassmann (2009) and D-whole's Membership clause.
- **Part / member:** a part of a whole is a member. It is inside the boundary and shares a fate with the whole. In a people, the members are persons and groups, and the organs are groups (A4).
- **Guest:** something that works beside or inside a whole without sharing its fate. A guest can become a member.
- **Degree of wholeness:** a position on the two dimensions (selectivity, and low conflict among members with interests), with no threshold. Where the parts have no interests, conflict is undefined. It is not a degree of freedom, and not a degree of confidence.
- **Vantage:** where a view is taken from. For a whole, either among its members or at a distance (D-whole, Vantage); both views are partial and both are true. More widely, the place any knower stands (70). Distinct from inside/outside.
- **Function / work:** what a whole does that contributes to keeping itself going (organizational account, Mossio, Saborido & Moreno 2009). Never a purpose.
- **The seven works:** the seven kinds of function A3 names: boundary, control (steering), energy (feeding), transport (carrying), signaling, defense, and memory. A3 claims they are performed; PR1 says why they should be. They are not a third dimension of wholeness.
- **Row:** one work traced across levels (cell, person, people) in A3. Each row is judged on its own evidence, and A3 fails if any row fails (39).
- **Pattern (PR1):** the recurrence of the same work across scales, because the same requirement holds. It explains; it doesn't raise scores.
- **Organization:** the arrangement that persists while a whole's matter turns over ("the shape," 25).
- **People:** a human society taken as a candidate whole (A3). The entries say "society"; the verses say "a people" for the same thing, and use "society" only in reporting a thinker who uses it.
- **Collective mind:** integration, decision and memory happening in a people as one system, emerging from feedback among its members (P1; 115). A process, not a thing, and not a claim that the people is conscious.
- **Signal:** a difference that makes a difference to a receiver, lessening its doubt.
- **Affect:** a change in the body's power of acting, with the idea of that change (E3D3). A **feeling** is the felt (inside) side of an affect. A **passion** is an affect of which we are only a partial cause.
- **Adequate cause:** a cause through which its effect can be clearly and distinctly perceived (E3D1). We *act* when we are the adequate cause, and are *passive* when we are only a partial cause (E3D2).
- **Integration:** the weaving of many signals into one act. A choice is an integration. Its felt side is the inside description; its outside description is a cause in the chain. The triple network (84) is a candidate neural correlate of integration.
- **Inside / outside description:** the two true descriptions of one event. *Inside* is the felt, first-person side (Nagel's sense). *Outside* is the causal chain as any observer could describe it. Neither outranks the other.
- **Point of view (functional):** a place an opinion comes from, which even the word "subjective" concedes (138). It is not the felt inside, and it settles nothing about feeling.
- **Freedom:** strictly, existing and acting from one's own nature alone, which only God does (E1D7, E1P17 Corollary 2). For finite things it is the **degree of adequate causation**, and only that (87). That clear understanding raises this degree is argued (95), not defined.
- **Responsibility:** with no one their own cause, not ultimate. It looks forward: to repair, restraint and learning (92).
- **Confidence / cap / score:** how sure the book is of a claim, as a ranking capped by the weakest support. Never a measure of wholeness. No scores are valid until the collider runs.
- **Undecided:** a state a claim can rest in when evidence runs out. Not a failure.
- **Definition vs axiom:** definitions (1, 2, 8, D-whole) fix meanings and support nothing alone; they were found downstream and placed first (151). Axioms (A2–A4, and the candidates 3 and 4) carry evidence and can be lowered. A1 is the root, the only unrevisable start.
- **Thesis:** every choice is one event with two true descriptions, outside and inside, and neither outranks the other (82). It rests on entries 5a, 5b and 6a. Freedom by degrees (87) and the replies to objections (83, 90, 91, 92) are consequences. The neural correlate (84, entry 6b) is a separate conjecture, not part of the thesis.
