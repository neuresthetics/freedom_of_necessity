# The hard problem, worked in the geometric manner (candidate, 2026-10-17)

A working draft next to the v12 verses and the [consciousness landscape note](v12_consciousness_landscape.md). It is not book text and not core. Nothing in `entries/` or in the verses is changed by it. Every id below is a **candidate** (prefix `C-HP-`). No scores are given: the collider has not run, so where a confidence is mentioned it is only the cap that the supports would set (METHOD rule 1). Kinds follow the book: *definition* (no confidence), *self-evident*, *empirical*, *model*, *declared reading* (unscored, adds nothing, METHOD rule 6). Grokipedia pages are cited by article title and sentence id from the 2026-10-17 snapshots; they are the pages' claims, not checked here.

The aim, as the author put it: to see whether the geometric method can solve the hard problem. The honest short answer this draft reaches is: it can **split** it, **dissolve** one part on the book's own reading, **prove** that a second part is not evidence for anything, and **locate** a third part exactly. It does not make the third part go away.

---

## 0. The problem, stated fairly

Chalmers (1995) separates the "easy" problems (explaining discrimination, report, attention, control) from the hard one: why the performance of those functions is accompanied by experience at all. The pages put it as explaining "why and how physical processes in the brain are accompanied by subjective, qualitative aspects of experience" and as "an apparent explanatory gap between third-person accounts of neural activity and first-person phenomenology" (*Hard problem of consciousness* C0001–C0002). Nagel (1974): an organism has conscious states only if there is "something that it is like to be that organism" (*Hard problem* C0110). Levine (1983) named the explanatory gap.

Read carefully, "the hard problem" bundles three different demands:

- **(Q1) Accompaniment.** Why is this physical process *accompanied by* experience?
- **(Q2) Derivation.** Why can't the first-person description be derived, a priori, from a complete third-person description? (Mary, the zombie and the bat all press this.)
- **(Q3) Distribution and existence.** Why does *this* organization have a first-person description at all, and which other things do?

The book's tools bear on each differently. Treating them as one question is part of why the problem looks seamless.

---

## 1. Definitions (candidates)

**C-HP-D1 (third-person description).** A description of a thing in terms any observer could in principle record and check, without being the thing: cells, signals, chemistry, behaviour, report. *Definition.* (This is A2's own sense, made explicit.)

**C-HP-D2 (first-person description).** A description of an organization *as it is for the one whose organization it is*: given to that one by being undergone, not by being observed. *Definition.* (A2's sense, with the mode of access stated.)

**C-HP-D3 (felt, thin sense).** An activity is *felt* when there is a true first-person description of it: when it is a seeming *for* the one doing it. This is Nagel's "for the organism" (*Hard problem* C0110), and only that. It says nothing about how rich, vivid or reportable the seeming is. *Definition.*

**C-HP-D4 (derivable).** A description B is *derivable* from a description A when someone who fully understands A, and has only A, can come to know B by reasoning alone. *Definition.* (This is the sense Mary and the zombie argument need: *Hard problem* C0084, C0151.)

**C-HP-D5 (accompaniment).** X *accompanies* Y when X and Y are two things and Y's occurrence is lawfully tied to X's. *Definition.* Accompaniment has two relata by its meaning.

Note on D3 and gap 4 (thinking vs feeling in D-I). D-I counts "sensory perceptions" only as *seeming* to perceive (D-I Explanation, after AT VII 29: "I certainly seem to see, to hear, and to be warmed"). A seeming is a seeming for someone. So D-I, without being defined through awareness, already lists at least one activity that, whenever it occurs, is felt in D3's thin sense. That is the bridge C-HP-P2 builds.

## 2. Axioms and readings used

Nothing new is assumed beyond the core. The draft uses:

- **A1** (self-evident, 1.0): I exist; this thinking is going on now.
- **D-I** (definition), including seeming to perceive.
- **A2 (a)** (empirical, pending): this thinking goes with this body and varies with its organization.
- **A2 (b)** (declared reading, unscored): my mind is that same organization described in the first person; one thing, two descriptions (after E2P7 S, E2P13, E3P2 S).

One further premise is named so nothing rides in unseen:

**C-HP-A1 (observer-availability).** What a third-person description records is, by D1, available to an observer who is not the thing; what a first-person description gives is, by D2, given by being undergone. These are two modes of access, and neither is defined in terms of the other. *Definition-level premise*: it restates D1 and D2 together. It carries no confidence and claims nothing about what exists.

## 3. Propositions (candidates), with demonstrations

### C-HP-P1. In my own case, the first-person description has standing that no third-person argument can supply or remove.

*Kind:* self-evident, for the author only. *Supports:* A1 (1.0), D-I, D2. *Cap:* 1.0, for whoever asserts it of themselves; for a reader, evidence only (A1, "For whom").

*Demonstration.* (1) By A1, this thinking is going on now, certain to me. (2) By D-I, the thinking A1 secures may be a seeming (I seem to see). (3) That something seems to me is given to me by its being undergone, so it is a first-person description in D2's sense, and it is true. (4) No third-person description can *supply* it: what a third-person description gives, it gives to any observer (D1), and A1's certainty is had only by the one asserting it (A1). (5) None can *remove* it: to deny that anything seems to me, I must take the denial to be so, which is itself a case of thinking going on (A1). ∎

*Scholium (what this does and does not show).* It refutes the claim that *nothing* seems: the strongest eliminativism. It does not refute illusionism as its best defenders state it. Frankish and Dennett grant that things seem; they deny that the seemings have the special properties the hard problem ascribes (intrinsic, ineffable, private qualia) (*Hard problem* C0156, C0159). C-HP-P1 secures seemings, not qualia in that heavy sense. And it says nothing about whether the seeming is a second thing or the organization itself: dualist and identity theorist alike accept it.

### C-HP-P2. For the author, some of A1's thinking is felt (thin sense).

*Kind:* self-evident, for the author only. *Supports:* C-HP-P1, C-HP-D3. *Cap:* as P1.

*Demonstration.* By P1, some of my thinking has a true first-person description. By D3, that is what "felt" means in the thin sense. ∎

*Scholium (gap 4).* This closes the gap between D-I and A2 *in the author's case* without changing D-I. D-I stays neutral about feeling for any other thinker (the AI question, D-I Scholium 2, stays open). What D-I left out by definition, A1 supplies by instance: my doubting is had. It does not show that all thinking is felt, nor that anything felt is rich or reportable.

### C-HP-P3. No first-person description, taken as first-person, is derivable from any third-person description.

*Kind:* derived from definitions; no evidence used. *Supports:* C-HP-D1, D2, D4, C-HP-A1. *Cap:* none from evidence; it stands or falls with the definitions being coherent.

*Demonstration.* (1) A first-person description gives its content by being undergone (D2). (2) A complete third-person description gives everything it gives to an observer who is not the thing (D1). (3) Knowing B "by reasoning alone from A" (D4) adds no new mode of access; it only rearranges what A gives. (4) So reasoning from A can yield, at most, a third-person description of the undergoing: that such-and-such organization is going on, that it would be reported as red, that it is the one the subject means by "this". (5) It cannot yield the content *in the mode* D2 names, since that mode is defined as not being observer-given (A1 premise). ∎

*Scholium 1 (it is the indexical gap, made general).* Perry (1979): someone can know every impersonal fact about a shopper spilling sugar and still not know "*I* am making a mess." No one takes that to show a non-physical shopper. A first-person description is the limiting case: an indexical that is not only *said* from here but *given* from here.

*Scholium 2 (falsifier, set up front).* P3 is lowered if anyone, from third-person description alone, comes to know a first-person content in D2's mode: Mary, still in the room, correctly anticipating and recognizing red by reasoning; or a scientist, from a full account of echolocation, coming to know what it is like for the bat. Note that the opponents of the book *agree* with P3 (*Hard problem* C0107, C0151). That agreement is what P4 uses.

### C-HP-P4. The explanatory gap is not evidence for a second thing.

*Kind:* derived. *Supports:* C-HP-P3, A2 (b) taken only as a hypothesis to be compared, METHOD rule 2. *Cap:* as P3.

*Demonstration.* (1) By P3, non-derivability follows from what a first-person description *is*, whatever it describes. (2) So if mind and organization are one thing described two ways (A2 (b)), the first-person description is still not derivable from the third-person one. (3) If they are two things (dualism), it is also not derivable. (4) A finding predicted equally by two hypotheses cannot raise one against the other (METHOD rule 2, read as: evidence moves a claim only against a rival that predicts otherwise). (5) So the gap, by itself, decides nothing between one thing and two. ∎

*Scholium.* This is the main result. The hard problem draws its force from the gap: "the third person leaves something out, so something is left over." P4 shows the first half is guaranteed by the two modes of access and so cannot carry the second. It is the same point Type-B materialists make with phenomenal concepts (*Hard problem* C0171–C0176; Loar 1990), reached here from the book's own definitions rather than from a theory of concepts.

### C-HP-P5. On the declared reading, Q1 (accompaniment) does not arise.

*Kind:* conditional on a declared reading; unscored. *Supports:* A2 (b), C-HP-D5. *Cap:* none; a declared reading adds no confidence, so P5 is a consequence of the reading, not a finding.

*Demonstration.* Accompaniment needs two relata (D5). On A2 (b), the felt and the organization are one thing told two ways. So on the reading there is nothing for the organization to be accompanied *by*; "why is it accompanied?" has a false presupposition, as "why does the morning star accompany the evening star?" does. ∎

*Scholium.* Q1 is *dissolved*, not answered, and only for someone who takes the reading. Anyone who rejects A2 (b) keeps Q1, and P5 gives them no reason to drop it. That is honest and it is limited.

### C-HP-P6. What remains of the hard problem on the reading is Q3, and Q3 has two parts of different kinds.

*Kind:* analysis; no confidence.

- **Q3a, distribution: which organizations have a first-person description?** This is an empirical and model question, of the same shape as A3. It is answerable in principle, and the field's theories (workspace, integrated information, higher-order, predictive) are rival answers to it (*Global workspace theory* C0001–C0004; *Integrated information theory* C0001; *Higher-order theories of consciousness* C0001).
- **Q3b, existence: why does Nature contain first-person descriptions at all?** This is not a question any proposition in the chain can answer, because any answer would be a third-person derivation (P3), or would posit something besides Nature (excluded by D-Nature). It has the same form as "why is there anything?" Spinoza does not derive that man thinks; he takes it as an axiom (E2 Axiom 2). The book's A1 does the same, more thinly.

*Demonstration of the split.* Q3a asks for a sorting of things and can be tested against cases, so it falls under the book's model kind. Q3b asks for a reason the first person exists that is not itself a description in one of the two modes; by P3 the third-person mode cannot deliver it, and the first-person mode only *has* it (P1). ∎

*Scholium.* So the geometric treatment ends where every geometric treatment ends: at its axioms. On this view the hard problem's last part is not a gap in the chain but the chain's *starting point*. A1 is "thinking is going on"; Q3b asks why that is so. The book already declines to derive its root. That is a relocation, and the draft says so plainly.

### C-HP-P7 (model, ‡). The first-person description covers the body's control work done as one, not the whole organization.

*Kind:* model; pending; ‡ (no entry, no evidence written in yet). *Supports:* D-work (control: combining signals and memory into one coordinated change), A2 (a), v124 ("Most of what I do never reaches it"). *Cap:* at most A2 (a), and lower until its own evidence is written.

*Statement.* Of this body's organization, what has a first-person description is what its control work takes as one: signals and memory combined into one coordinated change available to steer the whole. Bone remodelling, most immune signalling and much of the nervous system's own work are organization without a first-person description.

*Why it is offered.* It answers gap 1 in the book's own vocabulary, without naming a mechanism. It sits close to workspace theories (broadcast to many processes, *Global workspace theory* C0001–C0004) but is stated as a work, not a circuit, so rival mechanisms can compete to fill it.

*Falsifier, set up front.* Lowered by robust evidence of first-person contents with no involvement of the integrative control the body uses to steer (for example, felt contents shown independent of any whole-body coordination), or by integrative control demonstrably running with no first-person description in the same body. The difficulty, stated: nearly all such evidence comes through report, and report is itself control work, so the test is easy to fool (v162).

*Scholium (gap 2, combination).* If P7 holds, a first person goes with control done as one *at a level*. A cell steers at its own level, so P7 does not exclude a cell's first person, and the book's levels would then raise the combination problem (*Panpsychism* C0211). P7 does not settle this. An exclusion rule like IIT's (*Integrated information theory* C0129) is one option; leaving it undecided is the other. The draft leaves it undecided.

## 4. The collider: the opposing side at full strength

### 4.1 The zombie argument

*Falsifier for the book's side, set up front.* If it can be shown that, for consciousness, ideal conceivability entails possibility, then a zombie world is possible, and A2 (b)'s identity is false (not merely unscored).

*The argument, steelmanned* (Chalmers 1996; *Hard problem* C0082–C0085). (1) A world physically identical to ours with no experience is ideally conceivable: no contradiction appears however fully one understands both descriptions. (2) Ideal conceivability entails possibility. (3) So such a world is possible. (4) So experience is not necessitated by the physical, and physicalism is false.

*The opponent's best answer to P3 and P4.* "Yes, the gap is guaranteed by the two modes; that is my point. For water and H₂O, the a posteriori identity can be explained: 'water' picks out its referent by contingent marks (wet, clear), so we can conceive water without H₂O only by conceiving those marks without H₂O. 'Pain' picks out its referent *by how it feels*. There is no appearance/reality gap for the feeling itself. So conceiving the physical without the feeling is conceiving the physical without *pain*, not without a mark of it. The two-mode story explains why the gap looks the same either way, but in the pain case it cannot explain why conceivability fails to track possibility" (Chalmers 2007's dilemma for phenomenal-concept strategies is in this spirit).

*The book's reply.* The zombie case is conceivable *because of* P3: from a third-person description, the first-person mode is never reached, so nothing in the third-person description ever contradicts its absence. Conceivability here tracks the structure of the two modes, not the structure of the world. Premise (2) is plausible where both descriptions are third-person. Where one description is given only by being undergone, the zombie thinker imagines the third-person description and *fails to apply* the first-person one; the failure to apply a mode is not the imagining of an absence.

*Honest state.* This is a standoff at one premise: does ideal conceivability entail possibility when one side of the identity is given in the first-person mode? Neither side has a demonstration that the other must accept. **Undecided.** The geometric gain is that the whole dispute is pinned to that one premise.

### 4.2 The knowledge argument (Mary)

*Falsifier, set up front.* If Mary, on leaving the room, learns a *fact* (a true proposition not entailed by all the physical truths), then not every fact is physical, and A2 (b)'s "one thing" is false at least for facts.

*The argument, steelmanned* (Jackson 1982; *Hard problem* C0146–C0151). Mary knows every physical truth about colour vision. On seeing red she learns something: what it is like to see red. So there are truths that are not physical truths.

*The book's reply* (P3 applied). Mary acquires a new first-person description of an organization she already knew in the third person: a new mode, the same thing. P3 predicts she cannot have it beforehand, whatever she knows. So the novelty is predicted by both readings (P4) and decides nothing. This is the old-fact/new-mode reply; Lewis's ability reply (Lewis 1988) is a cousin.

*The opponent's best rejoinder.* "A new mode of presentation must itself be presented by some property. If that property is physical, Mary already knew it. If not, there is a non-physical property after all." The book's answer is that the mode is not a property *presented to* Mary but Mary's *undergoing* the organization (D2): not a further object, but being the thing rather than observing it. Whether that is an answer or a relabelling is exactly what the opponent disputes. **Undecided, leaning to the book** only in the narrow sense that the knowledge argument, unlike the zombie argument, needs the extra step that a new mode implies a new fact, and P3 explains the newness without it.

### 4.3 The strongest objection to the whole draft

**"Two descriptions" presupposes what it was meant to explain.** A first-person description is "the organization *as it is for* the one whose organization it is" (D2). But "as it is for" someone is the very phenomenality in question. So the double-description view does not explain why there is something it is like; it builds it into the second description and then calls the problem dissolved. Dual-aspect theories have long faced this (*Hard problem* C0199, C0215).

*Reply.* Granted, and the draft does not deny it. That is why P6 ends at Q3b: the first-person mode is not derived; it is taken as given, as A1 takes thinking as given. The draft claims to have shown that the hard problem's *argument* (from the gap to a second thing) fails, and that what is left is not a hidden step but the chain's starting point. It does not claim to have explained the starting point.

### 4.4 A second objection, from the other side (illusionism)

"You keep the first person as a real level only by assuming seemings have special status. Explain the *judgments* that there is a hard problem, and nothing is left over" (*Attention schema theory* C0060, C0064; Chalmers 2018 calls this the meta-problem). *Reply.* The book should welcome this as Q3a work: on A2 (b), our judgments about consciousness are caused by the same organization they describe, so a full third-person account of those judgments is predicted, not threatening. Where the book parts from illusionism is C-HP-P1: for the author, the seeming itself is not a judgment that could be wholly mistaken. Against the best illusionism this is again a standoff over what "seeming" includes.

What the geometric method actually bought: it separated three questions the phrase "hard problem" runs together; it showed that the one carrying the argumentative weight (the gap) follows from definitions and so cannot be evidence; and it pinned the remaining disputes to single, named premises. That is real progress by the method's own standards. It is not a solution of "why is anything felt", and no step above claims to be.

## 6. What would change in the core (proposals only)

None of these rewrites the core; each makes it more coherent, within the one chain.

1. **A2, "Limit" paragraph.** Add two sentences: the hard problem bundles three questions; on reading (b) the accompaniment question does not arise, and the missing derivation is what two descriptions predict, so it favours neither reading; why there is any first-person description at all stays undecided. (Uses C-HP-P3–P6; adds no confidence.)
2. **D-I, one sentence in "What D-I leaves out".** D-I does not define thinking through feeling; but seeming to perceive is on its list, and in the author's own case A1's thinking is had, so the A1 thinker feels in the thin sense; for any other thinker D-I stays neutral. (C-HP-P2.)
3. **A2, a line on which organization** (gap 1): "the first-person description covers what this body's control work takes as one; most of its organization never enters it; which structures do this is left to evidence." Tag ‡ until evidence is written. (C-HP-P7.)
4. **Later, if the author wants it: one entry, placed after A2**, a proposition carrying C-HP-P3/P4 (the gap is predicted by the two descriptions and so is not evidence), citing D-I, A1, A2. Same chain, no separate track. A collider pair on A2 (b), with §4.1 and §4.2 as the opposing side and their falsifiers as written, should run before any of this is promoted.

## 7. Draft verses (v12 style, candidates)

A. The third person tells what anyone could see. The first person tells what is had. Neither is made from the other. [C-HP-P3; A2, reading]

B. No account from outside will hand me red. That was never a sign of a second thing; it is what two descriptions of one thing would leave. [C-HP-P4]

C. To ask why the body is joined by a feeling is to count two. On my reading there is one, told two ways, and nothing to join. [C-HP-P5; A2, reading]

D. To deny that anything seems to me, I must take the denial to be so. Something seems. Whether it is more than the body, that does not decide. [C-HP-P1; A1]

E. My doubting is had, not only done. So the thinker of the start feels, in my own case. For others, the start says nothing. [C-HP-P2; A1, D-I]

F. ‡ Most of the body's work never reaches the first person. What reaches it is what is taken as one to steer. [C-HP-P7, ‡]

G. A world like this one with no one home: it can be pictured because the picture is drawn from outside. Whether it could be, undecided. [§4.1, scholium]

H. Why there is any first person at all, I don't derive. It is where I start. [C-HP-P6; A1]
