# A3: Isomorphism Table (Cell | Person | Society), sourced draft

**Entry A3 (model tier):** *Society exhibits organism-level properties across N rows.*
**Rule:** confidence(A3) = min(row scores).
**Used by:** A4, "I function within that society as a unit analogous to a cell" (cites A1 and A3).

> **Status of this draft.** The wording refinements, scores, and disanalogies below are *proposals for the author*. They are not settled results. Each score rates how strong the **structural or functional** correspondence is across all three levels, not how vivid the metaphor is. Every citation was checked against a publisher, DOI registry, library catalogue, or full-text source. The one exception is flagged in Section 6.

---

## 0. Framing notes that apply to every row

1. **There is a real precedent for a cross-level table.** James Grier Miller's *Living Systems* (1978) argues that living systems at seven levels (cell, organ, organism, group, organization, society, supranational system) are open systems made of the same **19 critical subsystems**. Examples include *boundary*, *distributor*, *converter*, *channel and net*, *memory*, and *decider*. A 20th subsystem, the *timer*, was added in 1990, and an eighth level, *community*, was added later. Miller's own word for the way the same subsystems re-appear at each higher level is "shred-out." Several rows below cite Miller's subsystem definitions, which gives the table a published backbone rather than a free-standing analogy.
2. **The book's three-level table skips levels.** In Miller's hierarchy a person (organism) is **not** a direct component of a society. Organisms make up groups, groups make up organizations, and organizations make up societies. The Cell | Person | Society columns therefore compress several levels. This matters for A4 (see Section 4).
3. **Miller himself limits the insect analogy.** In a later summary, Miller & Miller write that the "societies" of social insects are structurally closer to human *groups* than to human organizations or societies. Superorganism literature on insects (Wheeler 1911; Hölldobler & Wilson 2009) supports A3 only indirectly for human societies.
4. **The master disanalogy is conflict.** Queller & Strassmann (2009) define organismality as *high cooperation and low conflict among parts*. The cells of a body are (nearly) clonal and have strongly aligned interests. The members of a human society are not clonal, keep their own interests, and often come into conflict. Wilson & Sober (1989) and Sober & Wilson (1998) argue that groups *can* become organism-like when selection at the group level is strong enough. That is a matter of degree, not an identity. Every row inherits this caveat.
5. **Spencer's caveat on consciousness.** Spencer (1860), the classic statement of the "social organism," lists the differences himself. The most important one is that "the community as a whole has no corporate consciousness," while its members keep their own consciousness. He uses this to argue that citizens' welfare must not be sacrificed to the State. Spencer also warns against comparing society specifically to the *human* body. This bears directly on the Person column.
6. **A note for a public book.** Organismic analogies have been used rhetorically to justify subordinating individuals to the collective. Vidal (2024) makes this point in a Living Systems Theory context and recommends always pairing analogies with disanalogies. The disanalogy column below is written with that in mind.

## 2. Row-by-row

### Row 1: Boundary

**Refinement.** "Membrane" is correct. The cell **wall** is present only in bacteria, archaea, plants, and fungi, so it belongs here as structural support, not in the Defense row. For the Person column, "skin" should also include **mucosal surfaces** (gut, airways). These are the body's largest and most selective interfaces, and a closer match to a selectively permeable membrane than skin is. For the Society column, "borders, walls" captures only the physical boundary. Much of a society's boundary is informational: citizenship, language, legal jurisdiction, and controlled ports of entry.

**Sources.**
- Miller (1978), *Living Systems*. The **boundary** is one of only two subsystems that process both matter-energy and information. Its definition applies at every level: "the subsystem at the perimeter of a system that holds together the components which make up the system, protects them from environmental stresses, and excludes or permits entry to various sorts of matter-energy and information" (definition as reproduced in Miller & Miller, ISSS Primer). At the supranational level Miller names the boundary as forces "which defend, guard, or police" borders (p. 914, as quoted in secondary sources).
- Maturana & Varela (1980), *Autopoiesis and Cognition*. Treats the self-produced boundary as constitutive of a living unity. This supports the claim that a boundary is not incidental to organism-hood.

**Score: 0.75.** The same function (selective exchange plus holding components together) appears at all three levels, and Miller defines it cross-level.

**Strongest disanalogy.** A cell membrane is continuous and self-assembled. A society's boundary is discontinuous, partly symbolic, and constantly crossed by its own members, who can leave and join other societies. Spencer (1860) lists this dispersion and mobility of members among his main differences.

### Row 2: Control

**Refinement.** "Nucleus/DNA" as controller is misleading in two ways. (a) Prokaryotes have no nucleus but are fully regulated. (b) DNA is closer to a stored instruction set that is **read** by a distributed network of transcription factors, RNAs, and signaling proteins. Control lives in the **gene regulatory network**, not in one organelle. Suggested wording: *"gene regulatory and signaling networks (DNA read by transcription factors)."* For the Person column, "brain" is acceptable if it is described as the main **integrator** of a nervous system that also includes spinal reflexes, the autonomic system, and hormonal loops. For the Society column, "government, law" should be widened to "decision-making institutions." Markets, courts, and civil bodies also decide.

**Sources.**
- Miller (1978), *Living Systems*. The **decider** is "the executive subsystem which receives information inputs from all other subsystems and transmits to them information outputs that control the entire system." Miller & Miller add explicitly that this "does not imply that living systems always have a single executive component. Coordination and control are decentralized in many systems." This supports the distributed-control refinement at every level.
- Davidson & Levine (2008), "Properties of developmental gene regulatory networks," *PNAS*. Development is controlled by modular network subcircuits (feed-forward, feedback), not by a single locus.
- Bray (1995), "Protein molecules as computational elements in living cells," *Nature*. Signaling proteins form circuits that amplify, integrate, and store information. Cellular "decision-making" is spread across these circuits.

**Score: 0.60.** The functional role (integrating inputs and coordinating the whole) is real at all three levels. However, the architecture differs sharply: a distributed molecular network in the cell, a highly integrated neural hub in the person, and a plural set of competing institutions in society.

**Strongest disanalogy.** A society's "decider" is made of persons with their own goals, who can disobey, capture, or overthrow it. Neurons and transcription factors have no such independent agendas. Spencer (1860) compared Parliament to the cerebrum, but he also stressed that society has no corporate consciousness.

### Row 2a (sub-row under Control): Menon's triple network model, the book's free-will bridge

**Label:** *Free-will bridge (person-level mechanism of control; proposed society parallel).*

**Person level (well supported).**
- **Default mode network (DMN):** self-referential and internally directed thought. Main hubs are the posterior cingulate cortex (PCC) and medial prefrontal cortex (mPFC). It is typically deactivated during externally focused tasks (Raichle et al. 2001; Menon 2011).
- **Central executive network (CEN):** working memory, planning, and goal-directed control. Anchored in dorsolateral prefrontal cortex (dlPFC) and posterior parietal cortex (PPC) (Seeley et al. 2007; Menon 2011).
- **Salience network (SN):** detects behaviourally relevant events and **switches** between the DMN and CEN. Anchored in the anterior insula (fronto-insular cortex) and dorsal anterior cingulate cortex (dACC) (Seeley et al. 2007; Menon & Uddin 2010). Sridharan, Levitin & Menon (2008) used Granger-causality analysis of fMRI across three experiments. They report that the right fronto-insular cortex plays "a critical and causal role in switching between the CEN and the DMN."

**Proposed society parallels (the author's hypothesis; no source found).**
- DMN ↔ **collective identity and culture.** A society's self-model, narratives, and "resting" preoccupations.
- CEN ↔ **planning institutions.** Legislatures, agencies, firms' strategy units, science.
- SN ↔ **news, alarms, and crisis signals** that pull collective attention away from routine self-narration toward urgent problem-solving (and back).

**Sources (person level).** Menon (2011) *Trends Cogn Sci*; Sridharan, Levitin & Menon (2008) *PNAS*; Seeley et al. (2007) *J Neurosci*; Menon & Uddin (2010) *Brain Struct Funct*; Raichle et al. (2001) *PNAS*.

**Score: 0.35.** The person-level neuroscience is solid. However, (a) no published source that I could find maps the triple network onto societal institutions, so the society column is the author's own proposal; and (b) the Cell column is empty, so as it stands this is a two-level analogy, not a three-level isomorphism.

**Cautions.**
- Menon's model is a model of **large-scale network dynamics, attention and cognitive control, and psychopathology**. It does not itself make claims about free will. The book's "bridge" is an interpretive step by the author and should be marked that way.
- Granger causality on fMRI signals is an indirect, statistical notion of causation. "Causal" in Sridharan et al. should not be read as proof of mechanism.
- Menon's 2011 title reads "a **unifying** triple network model" (not "unified").

**Strongest disanalogy.** The three brain networks are anatomically fixed, and anti-correlated dynamics between them can be measured. Societal "attention" has no single measurable switch. Many salience channels (outlets, platforms, officials) compete, and they are often captured by interested parties.

**Recommendation.** Keep 2a as a named **bridge** under Control. Either (i) exclude it from the A3 minimum and give it its own entry and confidence, or (ii) include it, in which case A3 drops to 0.35. Option (i) seems cleaner, because 2a is a finer-grained claim than "society has a control subsystem."

### Row 3: Energy

**Refinement.** Mitochondria are found only in eukaryotic cells. Bacteria and archaea carry out respiration at the plasma membrane. Suggested cell wording: *"metabolism (glycolysis plus mitochondrial respiration in eukaryotes)."* In the Person column, "digestion" is really **ingestion and conversion** (Miller's ingestor and converter). The actual energy-releasing step, respiration, happens in the mitochondria of every body cell. So the person column should read *"digestion plus cellular respiration."* In the Society column, the power **grid** distributes energy rather than converting it, so it belongs in the Transport row. The conversion analogue is **power plants and fuel refining**, alongside agriculture (food energy for the human components).

**Sources.**
- Miller (1978). The **ingestor** "brings matter-energy across the system boundary"; the **converter** "changes certain inputs to the system into forms more useful for the special processes of that particular system." Both are defined cross-level.
- Lane & Martin (2010), "The energetics of genome complexity," *Nature*. Argues that mitochondria gave eukaryotic cells a large per-gene energy advantage. *Note: this hypothesis is contested (e.g. by Lynch & Marinov); cite it for the role of mitochondria in eukaryotic energetics, not as settled fact.*
- Bettencourt et al. (2007), *PNAS*. Compares cities with organisms and notes that organisms show sublinear metabolic scaling (β < 1). Cities show sublinear scaling for infrastructure but **superlinear** scaling (β ≈ 1.2) for socio-economic outputs, and a pace of life that **speeds up** with size.

**Score: 0.70.** Energy capture and conversion is a genuine, measurable function at all three levels, with cross-level quantitative work (scaling laws). It loses points because societal energy use is overwhelmingly external to the body (fossil and other fuels) rather than metabolic.

**Strongest disanalogy.** Organisms slow down per unit mass as they grow (sublinear metabolic scaling). Cities do the opposite for social and economic rates: the pace of life increases with size (Bettencourt et al. 2007).

### Row 4: Transport

**Refinement.** "Cytoplasm" alone implies passive transport, and in small cells much transport is indeed diffusion. Directed long-range transport in eukaryotic cells uses **motor proteins** (kinesins, dynein, myosin) walking along cytoskeletal tracks. Suggested wording: *"diffusion plus motor-driven transport along the cytoskeleton."* Add lymph to the person column. Add grids, pipelines, and rail to the society column.

**Sources.**
- Miller (1978). The **distributor** "carries inputs from outside the system or outputs from its subsystems around the system to each component," defined cross-level.
- Vale (2003), "The molecular motor toolbox for intracellular transport," *Cell*. Directed intracellular cargo transport by kinesin, dynein, and myosin motors.
- West, Brown & Enquist (1997), *Science*. Derives quarter-power metabolic scaling from space-filling, hierarchically branching distribution networks (e.g. the vascular system). *Note: the model's assumptions and derivation have been contested in later literature.*
- Bettencourt et al. (2007), *PNAS*. City infrastructure (e.g. electrical cable length, road surface) scales **sublinearly** (β ≈ 0.8), qualitatively like biological networks.
- Spencer (1860) compared roads and railways to blood vessels, including the observation that railways carry opposite-direction traffic "as do the arteries and veins."

**Score: 0.75.** This is one of the most studied cross-level correspondences. Both biological and urban distribution networks show economies of scale, and Miller treats the distributor as a universal subsystem.

**Strongest disanalogy.** Blood follows a closed, pump-driven loop with a single topology fixed by development. Human supply networks are open, many-to-many, redesigned deliberately, and routed by prices and decisions rather than pressure gradients.

### Row 5: Signaling (previously judged weakest; reassessed)

**Refinement.** The Person column merges two different systems: the **nervous system** (fast, point-to-point, "wired") and **hormones** (slow, broadcast through the blood). A tighter mapping splits the Society column the same way:
- nervous signaling ↔ **addressed telecommunications** (telephone, messaging, data networks);
- hormonal broadcast ↔ **mass media and public announcements**;
- local cell-to-cell (paracrine) signaling ↔ **face-to-face talk**;
- **prices** ↔ a distributed signal that coordinates parts without central control (Hayek 1945). This arguably resembles hormone or metabolite concentrations more than nerves.

"Language" is better treated as the **code** (Miller's encoder and decoder) than as a channel.

**Sources.**
- Miller (1978). The **channel and net** is "the subsystem composed of a single route in physical space, or multiple interconnected routes, by which markers bearing information are transmitted to all parts of the system." The encoder and decoder translate between private (internal) and public codes. All are defined cross-level.
- Bray (1995), *Nature*. Protein signaling circuits as the cell's information-processing machinery.
- Hayek (1945), "The Use of Knowledge in Society," *American Economic Review*. Describes the price system as a mechanism for communicating dispersed information, and compares it to a system of telecommunications.
- Maynard Smith & Szathmáry (1995). Count the origin of human **language** as a major evolutionary transition in how information is transmitted. This supports the special status of language at the society level.
- Spencer (1860) compared telegraph wires to nerves.

**Reassessment.** The previous judgment that signaling is the weakest row still holds, but only narrowly. The *function* (transmitting information that coordinates parts) is well supported at all three levels, and Miller defines it cross-level. The weakness is in the *content and integration* of the signals:
- Cellular and neural signals are interpreted mechanically by receptors.
- Societal signals are **symbolic** and are interpreted by autonomous agents, each with its own model of the world.
- Societal signals can be deliberately deceptive, and they flow through competing channels with no integrating centre.

**Score: 0.55.** The functional correspondence is real, but the society-level channels are multi-centred, semantic, and contested in ways that neural and cellular channels are not. On the proposed scores this is still the weakest main row.

**Strongest disanalogy.** In a body, signals converge on an integrating nervous system that serves the whole. A society has no single place where signals are integrated into one "view" (Spencer: no corporate consciousness), and many of its signals serve the interests of their senders rather than the whole.

### Row 6: Defense

**Refinement.** Replace "cell wall/enzymes." Animal cells have **no** cell wall, and the wall is structural (Row 1) rather than a defense system. Better cell-level entries are:
- the membrane barrier and receptor-level exclusion;
- **intrinsic, intracellular defenses**, such as autophagy that captures and degrades intracellular microbes, and the interferon and antiviral response of individual cells;
- **RNA interference** as an antiviral defense in plants and invertebrates;
- in bacteria, **restriction-modification systems** (restriction enzymes cut foreign DNA) and **CRISPR-Cas**, an adaptive, heritable immune memory.

For the Society column, "police, military" maps well onto a division between *internal* surveillance and *external* defense. **Public health** (quarantine, vaccination, sanitation) is the closest analogue to immunity in the strict sense of defense against pathogens. The best-studied example of collective pathogen defense is insect **social immunity**.

**Sources.**
- Labrie, Samson & Moineau (2010), "Bacteriophage resistance mechanisms," *Nature Reviews Microbiology*. Restriction-modification, CRISPR-Cas, and abortive infection (infected cells sacrificing themselves to protect the population) as cell-level defense.
- Levine, Mizushima & Virgin (2011), "Autophagy in immunity and inflammation," *Nature*. Autophagy as a cell-autonomous defense and a regulator of immunity.
- Cremer, Armitage & Schmid-Hempel (2007), "Social immunity," *Current Biology*. Colony-level anti-parasite defenses that arise from cooperative behaviour and organization. This is the strongest published society-level analogue.

**Score: 0.65.** Defense against invaders and internal defectors appears at all three levels. Bacterial "self-sacrifice" defenses and insect social immunity show striking convergence. The row loses points because (a) Miller's framework has no dedicated defense subsystem (Vidal 2024 notes that LST never treats the immune system systematically), and (b) the human-society mapping (police, military) is mostly about violence between agents rather than defense against pathogens.

**Strongest disanalogy.** The immune system is tuned to distinguish self from non-self by molecular markers that are, in principle, fixed. Societal "defense" decides who counts as a threat through politics and law, which change over time and can be turned against the society's own members.

### Row 7: Memory

**Refinement.** The draft mixes two timescales, and this weakens the row.
- **DNA** is primarily **cross-generational inheritance**, closer to what culture is for a society.
- **Personal memory** is **within-lifetime learning**. Its cell-level analogue is **epigenetic and regulatory-state memory**: a cell "remembers" its type through divisions via DNA methylation, chromatin states, and self-sustaining feedback loops.

Splitting the row into two sub-columns (within-lifetime and cross-generation) makes it much stronger. Writing and institutions then serve as the society's within-lifetime records, and culture and education as its cross-generational inheritance.

**Sources.**
- Miller (1978). **Memory** is "the subsystem which carries out the second stage of the learning process, storing various sorts of information in the system for different periods of time," defined cross-level. The **reproducer**, the subsystem that gives rise to similar systems, covers the cross-generational side.
- Bird (2002), "DNA methylation patterns and epigenetic memory," *Genes & Development*. DNA methylation as a stable cellular memory of gene-expression states.
- Jablonka & Lamb (2005), *Evolution in Four Dimensions*. Four inheritance systems: genetic, epigenetic, behavioural, and **symbolic** (language- and culture-based). This is effectively a published version of this row's three columns.
- Boyd & Richerson (1985), *Culture and the Evolutionary Process*. Culture as a second, non-genetic inheritance system with its own dynamics.

**Score: 0.65.** The storage-and-retrieval function is well supported at all three levels, and Jablonka & Lamb supply an explicit multi-level framework. The score is held down only by the timescale mixing in the original wording. With the split it could reasonably rise to about 0.7.

**Strongest disanalogy.** Cellular memory is copied with high fidelity and interpreted the same way by every cell. Societal memory is reinterpreted, contested, selectively forgotten, and rewritten by the agents who hold it.

## 4. Note on A4 placement

A4 ("I function within that society as a unit analogous to a cell") reads the table **diagonally**: at the society level, the person occupies the position the cell occupies at the person level. Three points for the author:

1. **Intermediate levels.** In Miller's hierarchy, persons are components of groups, which make up organizations, which make up communities and societies. A person is a cell-*like* unit of society only in a compressed three-level model. A4 should say "analogous to" (as it does) and ideally acknowledge the skipped levels.
2. **Autonomy and conflict.** Somatic cells are clonal, cannot leave, and have interests almost entirely aligned with the body (Queller & Strassmann 2009). Persons are genetically distinct, mobile, and conscious, and they keep their own interests (Spencer 1860). A more accurate cell-level model for a person may be a **free-living cell in a facultative colony** rather than a somatic cell. Such a cell cooperates, but could in principle survive or reorganize elsewhere.
3. **Normative caution.** Spencer drew the opposite normative conclusion from the one organismic language often invites: because society has no corporate consciousness, the State exists for the benefit of its members, not the reverse. If the book draws ethical conclusions from A4, it should state which direction it takes.
